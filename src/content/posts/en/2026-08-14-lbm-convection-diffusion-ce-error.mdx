In lattice Boltzmann, the diffusivity is fixed by a single relaxation time: $D = c_s^2(\tau - 1/2)\delta t$. Nothing to memorize. But notice what is missing from that line — velocity. Does the same $D$ come out once the flow is switched on? This post answers no. Under a uniform advection velocity $u$, the actual diffusivity drops to $D(1 - u^2/c_s^2)$, and at $u = 0.3$ that is 27% gone. We trace exactly where the Chapman–Enskog expansion drops the term, and how one source term brings it back.

The sting lands hardest in phase-field multiphase work. When Cahn–Hilliard or Allen–Cahn is solved on a lattice Boltzmann kernel, the interface thickness is tied directly to the mobility. A mobility that is 27% off gives an interface thickness that is off, and that in turn gives a surface tension coefficient that is off.

## The diffusivity is set, yet the interface keeps thinning

Start by looking at it. Below is the one-dimensional convection-diffusion equation

$$
\partial_t \phi + \partial_x(u\phi) = D\,\partial_{xx}\phi
$$

solved with a D1Q3 lattice Boltzmann scheme. The initial condition is a single Gaussian; the exact solution is also a Gaussian, widening as $\sigma^2(t) = \sigma_0^2 + 2Dt$. Try it directly in the simulation below.

Push the `u` slider to 0.30 and the solid curve (computed) rises above the dashed one (exact), sharper and taller. The $\sigma^2$ trace in the lower panel falls short of the dashed target slope. Drag `tau` anywhere you like and the deficit ratio refuses to move — that stubbornness is the whole story of this post.

## D1Q3 has only three moments to honor

Unlike a Navier–Stokes lattice Boltzmann scheme, a distribution $g_i$ built for scalar transport has fewer moments to satisfy. The equilibrium Guo used in his 2009 nonlinear convection-diffusion model is this:

$$
g_i^{eq} = w_i\,\phi\left(1 + \frac{\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\mathbf{u}}{c_s^2}\right)
$$

Here $w_i$ are the lattice weights, $\mathbf{c}_i$ the lattice velocities, and $c_s^2 = 1/3$ the squared lattice sound speed. It satisfies three moment constraints.

$$
\sum_i g_i^{eq} = \phi, \qquad \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i g_i^{eq} = \phi\mathbf{u}, \qquad \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i\mathbf{c}_i g_i^{eq} = c_s^2\phi\,\mathbf{I}
$$

The third one is where this parts ways with a flow equilibrium: there is no $\phi\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}$ term. The second-order term in velocity was never included. Why it can be dropped is the subject of [cutting the equilibrium down to Hermite polynomials](/en/posts/2026-05-25-lbm-equilibrium-hermite-d2q9/). In short, a scalar equation has no stress tensor, so an isotropic second moment is enough — which is also why D2Q5 can replace D2Q9 here.

That looks sufficient, and at $u = 0$ it is exact. The catch is that the truncation is not free.

## The fourth term Chapman-Enskog leaves behind

Write the BGK lattice Boltzmann equation with a source $S_i$ attached:

$$
g_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_i\delta t,\, t+\delta t) - g_i(\mathbf{x},t) = -\frac{1}{\tau}\left(g_i - g_i^{eq}\right) + \delta t\, S_i
$$

Expand $g_i = g_i^{(0)} + \varepsilon g_i^{(1)} + \varepsilon^2 g_i^{(2)}$ and split the time derivative as $\partial_t = \varepsilon\partial_{t_1} + \varepsilon^2\partial_{t_2}$. The zeroth moment at order $\varepsilon$ hands back the advective part of the target equation unchanged.

$$
\partial_{t_1}\phi + \nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf{u}) = 0
$$

The **first moment** of that same order-$\varepsilon$ equation is where everything is decided, because it fixes the flux carried by $g^{(1)}$.

$$
\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i g_i^{(1)} = -\tau\delta t\left[\partial_{t_1}(\phi\mathbf{u}) + c_s^2\nabla\phi - \sum_i \mathbf{c}_i S_i\right]
$$

The second term in the bracket, $c_s^2\nabla\phi$, is the diffusive flux we asked for. The first one is riding along with it. In a flow scheme the $\rho\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}$ term in the equilibrium second moment cancels most of it; the scalar model has no such term. So it survives.

Collecting the zeroth moment at order $\varepsilon^2$ gives the final form.

$$
\partial_t \phi + \nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf{u}) = \nabla\cdot(D\nabla\phi) + \nabla\cdot\left[\left(\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\delta t\,\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})\right] - \nabla\cdot\left[\tau\delta t\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i S_i\right]
$$

$D = c_s^2(\tau - 1/2)\delta t$ appears as expected. The second term on the right is a flux nobody ordered. It vanishes in a genuine steady state, where $u$ is constant in time and $\phi$ has stopped changing. But as long as $\phi$ is still moving — as long as the computation is running — $\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})$ is not zero.

## Under uniform $u$ that term is negative diffusion

Take the simplest case, $u$ constant in space and time. Then $\partial_t(\phi u) = u\,\partial_t\phi$, and to leading order $\partial_t\phi \simeq -u\,\partial_x\phi$. Substituting,

$$
\partial_x\left[\left(\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\delta t\,\partial_t(\phi u)\right] = -\left(\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\delta t\, u^2\,\partial_{xx}\phi
$$

A diffusion term with the sign reversed. Adding it to the physical one,

$$
\left(\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\delta t\left(c_s^2 - u^2\right)\partial_{xx}\phi = D\left(1 - \frac{u^2}{c_s^2}\right)\partial_{xx}\phi
$$

$$
D_{\text{eff}} = D\left(1 - \frac{u^2}{c_s^2}\right)
$$

Three things fall out at once. First, the deficit scales as $u^2$, so it hides at low speed — at $u = 0.05$ it is 0.75%. Second, $(\tau - 1/2)$ sits on both sides, so **the ratio is independent of $\tau$.** Turn up $\tau$ to get more diffusion and the spurious term grows by the same factor. Third, $D_{\text{eff}}$ crosses zero and goes negative as $u \to c_s$. Past that point the answer is not merely wrong, it blows up.

## $1 - 1/(2\tau)$ shows up again

Now fix the coefficient of $S_i$. For the spurious term and the source term to cancel in the equation above,

$$
\tau\delta t\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i S_i = \left(\tau-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\delta t\,\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})
$$

$$
\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i S_i = \left(1 - \frac{1}{2\tau}\right)\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})
$$

Only one simple form satisfies this while also keeping $\sum_i S_i = 0$.

$$
S_i = w_i\left(1 - \frac{1}{2\tau}\right)\frac{\mathbf{c}_i\cdot\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})}{c_s^2}
$$

There is $1 - 1/(2\tau)$ again. This factor grows from the same root as the one in [where half the force goes in LBM forcing schemes](/en/posts/2026-07-27-lbm-forcing-term-guo-shan-chen-edm/). On a discrete-time lattice a source acts twice — once through $g^{(1)}$, once through the second-order term of the Taylor expansion — and the factor of $1/2$ is the residue of that double counting.

What happens if you drop the coefficient and set $\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i S_i = \partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})$? At $\tau = 1$ you apply exactly twice the correction, and a 27% deficit becomes a 27% excess. The magnitude of the error is unchanged, so a log-log convergence plot will not catch it.

Computing $\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})$ in code means storing $\phi u$ from the previous step and taking a backward difference. One extra array is the entire cost.

## Measuring $D_{\text{eff}}$ in 60 lines of Python

Rather than argue, measure. Advect a Gaussian, fit the growth slope of its second moment $\sigma^2$ by least squares, and that slope is $2D_{\text{eff}}$.

```python
import numpy as np

CS2 = 1.0 / 3.0
C = np.array([0, 1, -1])
W = np.array([2 / 3, 1 / 6, 1 / 6])


def d1q3_equilibrium(phi, u):
    """g_i^eq = w_i phi (1 + c_i u / cs^2) — an equilibrium fixing only three moments"""
    return np.stack([W[i] * phi * (1.0 + C[i] * u / CS2) for i in range(3)])


def gaussian_moments(x, phi):
    m0 = phi.sum()
    mean = (x * phi).sum() / m0
    return mean, (((x - mean) ** 2) * phi).sum() / m0


def run_cde_lbm(L, steps, tau, u, sigma0, x0, corrected):
    x = np.arange(L, dtype=float)
    phi = np.exp(-((x - x0) ** 2) / (2 * sigma0**2))
    g = d1q3_equilibrium(phi, u)
    phi_old = phi.copy()
    hist = []
    for n in range(steps + 1):
        if n % 100 == 0:
            hist.append((n, gaussian_moments(x, phi)[1]))
        src = np.zeros_like(g)
        if corrected and n > 0:
            # S_i = w_i (1 - 1/(2 tau)) c_i d_t(phi u) / cs^2,  dt = 1
            dt_phiu = (1.0 - 1.0 / (2 * tau)) * u * (phi - phi_old)
            for i in range(3):
                src[i] = W[i] * C[i] * dt_phiu / CS2
        geq = d1q3_equilibrium(phi, u)
        g = g - (g - geq) / tau + src          # collision
        for i in range(3):
            g[i] = np.roll(g[i], C[i])         # streaming
        phi_old = phi
        phi = g.sum(axis=0)
    return np.array(hist)


def fit_diffusivity(hist):
    """read D_eff off the slope of sigma^2 = sigma0^2 + 2 D_eff t"""
    return np.polyfit(hist[:, 0], hist[:, 1], 1)[0] / 2.0


L, STEPS, SIG0, X0 = 800, 1600, 10.0, 80.0

tau = 1.0
D = CS2 * (tau - 0.5)
print(f"tau = {tau},  D = cs^2 (tau-1/2) = {D:.6f},  cs^2 = {CS2:.6f}")
print(f"{'u':>6} {'u^2/cs^2':>9} | {'D_eff (no src)':>14} {'ratio':>7} {'1-u^2/cs^2':>11} |"
      f" {'D_eff (src)':>12} {'ratio':>7}")
for u in [0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30]:
    d_raw = fit_diffusivity(run_cde_lbm(L, STEPS, tau, u, SIG0, X0, False))
    d_fix = fit_diffusivity(run_cde_lbm(L, STEPS, tau, u, SIG0, X0, True))
    print(f"{u:>6.2f} {u * u / CS2:>9.4f} | {d_raw:>14.6f} {d_raw / D:>7.4f} {1 - u * u / CS2:>11.4f} |"
          f" {d_fix:>12.6f} {d_fix / D:>7.4f}")

u = 0.25
print(f"\nu = {u} fixed, tau sweep   (theory: ratio = 1 - u^2/cs^2 = {1 - u * u / CS2:.4f}, tau-independent)")
print(f"{'tau':>6} {'D':>10} | {'D_eff (no src)':>14} {'ratio':>7} | {'D_eff (src)':>12} {'ratio':>7}")
for tau in [0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5]:
    D = CS2 * (tau - 0.5)
    d_raw = fit_diffusivity(run_cde_lbm(L, STEPS, tau, u, SIG0, X0, False))
    d_fix = fit_diffusivity(run_cde_lbm(L, STEPS, tau, u, SIG0, X0, True))
    print(f"{tau:>6.1f} {D:>10.6f} | {d_raw:>14.6f} {d_raw / D:>7.4f} | {d_fix:>12.6f} {d_fix / D:>7.4f}")
```

In the first table the `ratio` column matches `1-u^2/cs^2` to four decimal places. The prediction is not an estimate; it is the exact leading order. Switch the source term on and all four velocities return to 1.0000.

The second table bites harder. Sweeping $\tau$ from 0.6 to 1.5 changes $D$ by a factor of ten, and the deficit ratio moves only from 0.8129 to 0.8124. Trying to bury the error under a larger diffusivity does not work: raise $D$ tenfold and the amount that disappears rises tenfold too.

## The lattice velocity budget was already spent

Look at the shape $u^2/c_s^2$ once more. That is the squared lattice Mach number. The usual justification for keeping $u < 0.1$ in LBM is compressibility error. Scalar transport supplies a second reason. Flow and scalar are drawing on the same lattice velocity budget, and the scalar side's bill arrives much earlier.

Three regimes are worth separating in practice.

- **Low-speed diffusion, $u \le 0.05$.** Under 1% deficit, buried in the discretization error. Running without the source term is defensible.
- **Ordinary computations, $u \sim 0.1{-}0.2$.** A 3–12% deficit. If you plan to report an interface thickness or a Sherwood number quantitatively, turn it on.
- **Phase-field multiphase.** $u$ spikes locally near the interface, and $\partial_t u$ is not zero either. Of the two pieces of $\partial_t(\phi u)$, the $\phi\,\partial_t u$ half survives as well. The source term stops being optional.

That third case carries one more caveat. The spurious term is the full $\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})$, not something of the form $u^2$. The expression $u^2/c_s^2$ is a special solution valid only for uniform, steady $u$. Before dropping this into a multiphase code, difference $\partial_t(\phi\mathbf{u})$ directly, and note that [when relaxation times are split per moment as in MRT](/en/posts/2026-06-26-mrt-lbm-moment-space-collision/), the $\tau$ inside $1 - 1/(2\tau)$ is the one belonging to the first moment.

So if the interface keeps thinning or thickening and the mobility calculation checks out no matter how many times you review it, look outside the calculator. $\tau$ was right. The flow took the rest.
