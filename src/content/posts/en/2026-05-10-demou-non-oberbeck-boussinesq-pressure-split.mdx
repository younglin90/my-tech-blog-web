Gray and Giorgini left two numbers in 1976. For room-temperature air, Oberbeck–Boussinesq stays inside a 10% error only up to 28.6 K. For water, the limit is 1.25 K. Solar-tower air swings through hundreds of kelvins. PV/T water warms by 50 K. Demou, Frantzis and Grigoriadis (2018) drop the assumption. Properties follow temperature. Only the pressure Poisson stays constant-coefficient.

Move the hot and cold walls and, at the same ΔT, the density curve stays almost flat while the viscosity curve tips hard.

Across 50 K the density moves about 2%. Viscosity moves about 30% on that same interval, and that gap builds the asymmetric boundary layer.

## Paper card

- **Authors**: A. D. Demou, C. Frantzis, D. G. E. Grigoriadis
- **Journal**: *International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer* 121 (2018) 1148–1162
- **DOI**: [10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2018.04.144](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2018.04.144)
- **Contribution**: in non-OB natural convection, keep temperature-dependent properties and turn the variable-coefficient pressure Poisson into a constant-coefficient Poisson plus an explicit correction, by the Dodd–Ferrante split. The FFT solver stays. The reported speedup is 2.5–5×.

## Where Boussinesq actually breaks

Standard Boussinesq holds three statements at once. Density varies only in the gravity term. Every other property is constant. Viscous dissipation is dropped. Once ΔT grows, viscosity $\mu(T)$, conductivity $k(T)$, and specific heat $C_p(T)$ move together. In a liquid the density shift can be small while the viscosity shift is not.

Keep every property and the momentum equation picks up a variable coefficient.

$$
\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial(u_i u_j)}{\partial x_j} = -\frac{1}{\hat\rho}\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_i} + \frac{1}{\hat\rho}\frac{\Pr}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Ra}}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\!\left[\hat\mu\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right)\right] + \frac{1}{\mathrm{Fr}^2}\delta_{i3}
$$

Hats are properties normalized by the reference-temperature value. The same variability enters the energy equation.

## The variable Poisson trap

A plain fractional-step correction asks the projection step for a divergence-free field. That step is what comes after [Chorin removes pressure from the momentum equation](/en/posts/2026-07-03-chorin-projection-fractional-step/).

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\!\left(\frac{1}{\hat\rho^{n+1}}\frac{\partial P^{n+1}}{\partial x_i}\right) = \frac{1}{\Delta t}\frac{\partial u_i^*}{\partial x_i}.
$$

The factor $1/\hat\rho^{n+1}$ sits inside the divergence. When it varies in space and time, the matrix changes every step. An FFT direct solver accepts only a constant-coefficient Laplacian. The fallback is multigrid or an iterative solver. If 60–80% of a Navier–Stokes step is the pressure solve, that fallback is the bill.

## Dodd–Ferrante splitting — one line

The borrowed trick comes from two-fluid flow (Dodd & Ferrante 2014). The pressure gradient is cut in two.

$$
\frac{1}{\hat\rho^{n+1}}\frac{\partial P^{n+1}}{\partial x_i} \longrightarrow \frac{1}{\hat\rho_0}\frac{\partial P^{n+1}}{\partial x_i} + \left(\frac{1}{\hat\rho^{n+1}} - \frac{1}{\hat\rho_0}\right)\frac{\partial \widehat P}{\partial x_i}
$$

The first piece multiplies the new pressure by the constant $1/\hat\rho_0$. The second multiplies the extrapolated pressure $\widehat P = 2P^n - P^{n-1}$. Take the divergence and the Poisson is constant-coefficient.

$$
\frac{\partial^2 P^{n+1}}{\partial x_i \partial x_i} = \frac{\hat\rho_0}{\Delta t}\frac{\partial u_i^*}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\!\left[\left(1 - \frac{\hat\rho_0}{\hat\rho^{n+1}}\right)\frac{\partial \widehat P}{\partial x_i}\right]
$$

The Laplacian on the left is the same every step. FFT in two directions leaves a stack of Helmholtz problems in $z$, solved directly. Only the right-hand correction is explicit.

The size that matters is $(1 - \hat\rho_0/\hat\rho)$. It sits near 1 in two-fluid flow and is tiny in water NOB convection.

## A NumPy walk-through

Sections 2.2–2.3 are compressed to one 1D slab. No 2D DNS. One pressure correction.

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.fft import fft, ifft

# 1D slab: periodic, one Helmholtz stack
Nz = 64
Lz = 1.0
dz = Lz / Nz
z = (np.arange(Nz) + 0.5) * dz

# temperature-dependent density (water polynomial, simplified)
def rho_hat(T_field, T0=313.0):
    dT = T_field - T0
    return 1.0 * (1 - 3.736e-4 * dT - 3.98e-6 * dT**2)

# initial temperature profile: linear + small perturbation
T0 = 313.0
dT = 30.0
T_field = T0 - dT/2 + dT * z + 0.1 * np.sin(2*np.pi*z)
rho = rho_hat(T_field)

# reference density: the lightest (hottest) value
rho_ref = rho.min()

# fake divergence (in a full solver this comes from ∇·u* after the fractional step)
div_u_star = 0.01 * np.sin(2*np.pi*z)
dt = 1e-3

# extrapolated pressure (paper Eq. 15)
P_n_minus_1 = np.zeros(Nz)
P_n = 0.01 * np.cos(2*np.pi*z)
P_hat = 2 * P_n - P_n_minus_1
dP_hat = np.gradient(P_hat, dz)

# correction term (RHS second piece of Eq. 16)
correction = (1 - rho_ref / rho) * dP_hat
rhs = rho_ref / dt * div_u_star + np.gradient(correction, dz)

# constant-coefficient Laplacian solve (FFT, periodic BC)
k = 2*np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(Nz, dz)
laplacian_eigenvalues = -(k**2)
laplacian_eigenvalues[0] = 1.0  # remove null space
P_new = np.real(ifft(fft(rhs) / laplacian_eigenvalues))
P_new -= P_new.mean()

# size of the correction term
correction_magnitude = np.max(np.abs(1 - rho_ref / rho))
print(f"max |1 - rho_0/rho|  = {correction_magnitude:.4f}")
print(f"P_new range           = [{P_new.min():.3e}, {P_new.max():.3e}]")
```

The output is:

```text
max |1 - rho_0/rho|  = 0.0117
P_new range           = [-1.572e-06, 1.572e-06]
```

The default slider is water across 50 K. The correction stays inside 1%. Switch to the two-fluid range and the same term moves toward 0.5. The code's ΔT = 30 K print of 0.0117 is a narrower window than that 50 K slider.

The Gray–Giorgini water limit is 1.25 K. The code's ΔT = 30 K is $30/1.25 = 24$ times that limit. The air 10% line is 28.6 K, so 30 K just clears it. A PV/T gap of 50 K is 40 times the water limit. Over that 50 K, density moves about 2% and viscosity about 30%.

The linear coefficient in the density polynomial is $3.736\times 10^{-4}$. Times 30 K it is $3.736\times 10^{-4}\times 30 = 0.0112$, next to the printed $\max|1-\rho_0/\rho| = 0.0117$. The quadratic coefficient $3.98\times 10^{-6}$ times $(30/2)^2 = 225$ is about $9.0\times 10^{-4}$. Most of 0.0117 is the linear term. With $T_0 = 313\,\mathrm{K}$, $dt = 10^{-3}$ and a divergence amplitude of 0.01, the pressure range is $\pm 1.572\times 10^{-6}$.

The two-fluid example is $\rho_{\min} = 1$, $\rho_{\max} = 1000$. Then $1 - 1/1000 = 0.999$. That is two orders away from the water value 0.0117. When the pressure solve is 60–80% of a step, keeping the FFT is how the paper's 2.5–5× shows up. Watching only $\mathrm{CFL}_{\max} = 0.2$ misses $\mathrm{VSL} = \nu\Delta t/\Delta x^2$, which crosses 0.03 first. The theory allows 0.3, and the higher-order statistics are already off by 3.5%.

The sentence that the correction at ΔT = 30 K stays inside 1.2% points at the same printed 0.0117. The same code with $\rho_{\min} = 1$ and $\rho_{\max} = 1000$ jumps to 0.999. That is why a two-fluid solver using this split still asks for a small $\Delta t$.

With $N_z = 64$ and $L_z = 1$, $dz = 1/64$. The extrapolated pressure is $\widehat P = 2P^n - P^{n-1}$. In the listing the amplitude of $P^n$ is 0.01 and $P^{n-1}$ is 0, so the amplitude of $\widehat P$ is 0.02. The printed 0.0117 is the coefficient on that gradient: the explicit correction is 1.17% of it. Table 2 spans $Ra$ from $10^6$ to $10^8$. Loosening the VSL ceiling from 0.03 to 0.3 on that range shifts the higher-order statistics by 3.5%.

## Three pitfalls during verification

Table 2 (air DHC, $Ra = 10^6$ to $10^8$) bites in three places.

- **Choosing $\hat\rho_0$** — Dodd–Ferrante uses the *minimum* density, so $(1-\hat\rho_0/\hat\rho)$ stays at least 0 and below 1. The paper says a reference-temperature density gives almost the same NOB answer, but a large ΔT spends the stability margin.
- **CFL and VSL together** — explicit Adams–Bashforth means $\mathrm{CFL}_{\max} = 0.2$ is not the first limit. $\mathrm{VSL} = \nu\Delta t/\Delta x^2 < 0.03$ is the practical ceiling. Theory allows 0.3, and higher-order statistics drift by 3.5%.
- **Arakawa convective form** — momentum and kinetic energy have to be conserved together at the discrete level. Plain central differences leak energy, and long-time averages tilt at large Ra.

## Whether to pin rho_0 at the minimum density

Dodd–Ferrante pins $\hat\rho_0$ at the minimum density so that $(1-\hat\rho_0/\hat\rho)$ stays at least 0 and below 1. The paper says a reference-temperature density is close enough for NOB. Past ΔT = 30 K that gap spends the margin. The printed 0.0117 is a 1D slab with the minimum density. It is not the bill for a 2D DNS.

The 2.5–5× figure assumes the pressure solve is 60–80% of the step, and that an FFT has a periodic direction to eat. Explaining the asymmetric boundary layer by a 2% density change misses the 30% viscosity change. The temperature where that assumption closes is not the OB window in [natural convection at Rayleigh number 1708](/en/posts/2026-06-04-natural-convection-rayleigh-benard/). For water it is already closed at 1.25 K.
