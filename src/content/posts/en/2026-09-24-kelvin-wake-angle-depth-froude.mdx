## The Seine, 1803, and the problem of pushing a boat upstream

On 9 August 1803 Robert Fulton ran a steamboat on the Seine. The plaque on the riverbank names
three men who watched: Bossut, Carnot, Prony. All three were fluid dynamicists.

The steam engine had existed since 1776. Several people had already bolted one onto a boat.
One thing kept failing. The boats could not work their way upstream. What Fulton solved was not
the engine. It was the resistance.

This article takes one piece of that resistance apart: the geometry of the waves a hull drags
behind it. In deep water the wake half-angle refuses to move off 19.47 degrees no matter how hard
you push the boat. Drop the depth to 2 m and the same angle opens to 89 degrees. We will follow a
single dispersion relation all the way to that number.

## Resistance that had to be measured because nobody could compute it

In 1752 d'Alembert showed that a body in inviscid irrotational flow feels exactly zero drag.
Theory and reality had split open. The field lost its tool for predicting resistance.

Bossut filled the gap with towing-tank experiments. He pulled model hulls at fixed speed and read
the tension. Prony organised the data. What Fulton inherited was that experimental tradition.

Much of what they measured was not friction. It was energy that the boat's own waves carried away
behind it. Start with where those waves can exist.

## Only the waves that stand still survive

In a frame riding with the hull the wake is stationary. To look stationary, a wave's phase speed
must match the component of the ship's speed along its own direction of travel.

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the wavenumber vector and the ship's track. The condition reads:

$$
V\cos\theta = c(k) = \sqrt{\frac{g}{k}\tanh kh}
$$

$V$ is ship speed, $k$ wavenumber, $h$ depth, $g$ gravity. The right side is the surface-wave
dispersion relation.

That one equation carries the whole article. In deep water ($kh \gg 1$), $\tanh kh \to 1$ and
$c = \sqrt{g/k}$ — depth drops out. In shallow water ($kh \ll 1$), $\tanh kh \to kh$ and
$c \to \sqrt{gh}$ — wavenumber drops out. Everything interesting lives between those two limits.

Phase speed cannot beat $\sqrt{gh}$. With a finite bottom, no wave however
long can beat that speed. Once the ship exceeds it, there is no way left to balance the left side.

## Where the energy actually goes

Crests travel at the phase speed, but energy travels at the group velocity. In finite depth:

$$
c_g(k) = \frac{c}{2}\left(1 + \frac{2kh}{\sinh 2kh}\right)
$$

The bracket runs from 1 in deep water to 2 in shallow water, so $c_g$ ranges from $c/2$ up to $c$.

That group velocity builds the wake. Follow a wave component launched $\tau$ seconds ago. The ship
has advanced $V\tau$; the energy has run $c_g\tau$ along the wavenumber direction. Measured from
the ship, that component now sits $(V - c_g\cos\theta)\tau$ astern and $c_g\sin\theta \cdot \tau$
off to the side.

$\tau$ cancels. The direction in which a given component shows up does not depend on its age.

$$
\tan\alpha(\theta) = \frac{c_g\sin\theta}{V - c_g\cos\theta}
$$

$\alpha$ is the ray angle measured from the ship's track. The wedge angle is the maximum of
$\alpha$ as $\theta$ sweeps from $0$ to $90°$. Stationary phase selects exactly that direction,
energy piles up there, and it shows on the surface as a sharp boundary.

## Where 19.47 degrees comes from

Run the deep-water case. With $c = \sqrt{g/k}$, the stationary condition gives $c = V\cos\theta$
and $c_g = c/2 = V\cos\theta/2$. Substitute and $V$ cancels outright.

$$
\tan\alpha = \frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2 - \cos^2\theta}
$$

Speed has vanished from the expression. This function peaks at $\cos^2\theta = 2/3$, that is
$\theta = 35.26°$, where it equals $1/(2\sqrt{2})$. As an angle, $19.47°$.

Two metres per second or eight, the answer is the same. All speed changes is the wavelength. The
deep-water transverse wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi V^2/g$, quadratic in speed. The shape holds;
only the scale grows.

With $\cos^2\theta = 2/3$, $\cos\theta = \sqrt{2/3}$ and $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1/3}$. The numerator
is $\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sqrt{2}/3$ and the denominator is $2 - 2/3 = 4/3$. The ratio is
$\sqrt{2}/4 = 1/(2\sqrt{2})$. The arctangent of that is 19.47°. $V$ sits in both numerator and
denominator and cancels. At $g = 9.81$, $V = 2$ m/s gives $2\pi \times 4 / 9.81 = 2.56$ m.
Quadrupling speed to 8 m/s multiplies $V^2$ by 16 and the wavelength becomes 40.99 m. Doubling
speed to 4 m/s multiplies wavelength by 4, and 10.25 / 2.56 = 4. The table rows 2.56, 10.25 and
40.99 are that ratio.

## Dropping the depth to 2 m and rerunning the same calculation

Now keep $\tanh kh$ intact and solve numerically. Bisect the stationary condition for
$k(\theta)$, feed it to the group velocity to build $\alpha(\theta)$, then sweep for the maximum.

```python
import math

g = 9.81

def c_phase(k, h):
    return math.sqrt(g / k * math.tanh(k * h))

def solve_k(v, h):
    if v >= math.sqrt(g * h) - 1e-12:
        return None
    lo, hi = 1e-8, 1.0
    while c_phase(hi, h) > v:
        hi *= 2.0
        if hi > 1e6:
            return None
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if c_phase(mid, h) > v:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

def group_velocity(k, h):
    kh = k * h
    if kh > 20.0:
        f = 1.0
    else:
        f = 1.0 + 2.0 * kh / math.sinh(2.0 * kh)
    return 0.5 * c_phase(k, h) * f

def ray_angle(theta, v, h):
    k = solve_k(v * math.cos(theta), h)
    if k is None:
        return None
    cg = group_velocity(k, h)
    num = cg * math.sin(theta)
    den = v - cg * math.cos(theta)
    if den <= 0.0:
        return 0.5 * math.pi
    return math.atan2(num, den)

def wedge_scan(v, h, n=8000):
    best, best_t = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in range(1, n):
        th = 0.5 * math.pi * i / n
        a = ray_angle(th, v, h)
        if a is not None and a > best:
            best, best_t = a, th
    return math.degrees(best), math.degrees(best_t)

print("  V[m/s]   h[m]    Fr_h    wedge[deg]  theta*[deg]   lambda_t[m]")
cases = [(2.0, 500.0), (4.0, 500.0), (8.0, 500.0),
         (2.0, 2.0), (3.5, 2.0), (4.0, 2.0), (4.3, 2.0),
         (4.43, 2.0), (5.5, 2.0), (8.0, 2.0)]
for v, h in cases:
    frh = v / math.sqrt(g * h)
    a, t = wedge_scan(v, h)
    k0 = solve_k(v, h)
    lam = (2 * math.pi / k0) if k0 else float('inf')
    ls = f"{lam:10.2f}" if k0 else "       inf"
    print(f"{v:7.2f} {h:7.1f} {frh:7.3f} {a:11.2f} {t:11.2f} {ls}")

print()
print("supercritical branch vs Mach-like angle arcsin(1/Fr_h)")
print("   Fr_h    wedge[deg]   asin(1/Fr_h)[deg]   diff")
for v, h in [(5.0, 2.0), (5.5, 2.0), (6.5, 2.0), (8.0, 2.0)]:
    frh = v / math.sqrt(g * h)
    a, _ = wedge_scan(v, h)
    m = math.degrees(math.asin(1.0 / frh))
    print(f"{frh:7.3f} {a:11.2f} {m:17.2f} {a-m:8.3f}")
```

The first three rows are deep water. Quadruple the speed and the wedge angle agrees to two decimal
places. Only the wavelength moves, from 2.56 m to 40.99 m — a factor of 16.

Row four is the interesting one. Even at $h = 2$ m the answer is still 19.47 degrees, with
Fr_h = 0.452. A short 2.56 m wave gives $kh = 4.9$, so as far as that wave is concerned the water
is deep. What matters is depth relative to wavelength, not depth on its own.

With $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $h = 2$ m and $\lambda = 2.56$ m give $kh = 2\pi \times 2 / 2.56 = 4.9$.
$\tanh(4.9)$ sits on 1, so the dispersion relation collapses back to the deep-water form. That is
why a 2 m bottom still returns 19.47°. At $V = 4.30$ m/s and $\lambda = 29.17$ m,
$kh = 2\pi \times 2 / 29.17$ falls to about 0.43. $\tanh kh$ leaves 1 and the table angle is
54.46°. The rows run 23.74° at Fr_h = 0.790, 35.13° at 0.903, 54.46° at 0.971 and 89.08° at
1.000. An angle that refused to move when speed was quadrupled in deep water opens from 23.74° to
89.08° between 4.30 m/s and 4.43 m/s.

Past Fr_h of about 0.79 the angle starts to move: 35.13 degrees at 0.903, 54.46 at 0.971, and
89.08 at 1.000. That last value falls 0.9 degrees short of 90 because the $\theta$ grid is finite.
Read it together with $\theta^*$, which has collapsed to 0.91 degrees. The peak is converging on
$\theta = 0$.

## Above the critical speed the wedge narrows again

Once Fr_h exceeds 1, only directions satisfying $V\cos\theta \le \sqrt{gh}$ survive — that is,
$\theta > \arccos(1/\mathrm{Fr}_h)$. A forbidden band opens up ahead of the beam. Transverse waves
disappear entirely, which is what the inf entries in the $\lambda_t$ column mean.

The wedge built from the surviving divergent waves narrows: 53.63 degrees at 1.242, 33.61 at 1.806.
The second table checks those against $\arcsin(1/\mathrm{Fr}_h)$. All four cases agree to within
0.015 degrees.

This is the same formula as the Mach cone in supersonic flow, with $\sqrt{gh}$ standing in for the
sound speed. Transverse waves vanishing once Fr_h passes 1, leaving only divergent waves, is the
same split
[a hydraulic jump in a kitchen sink](/en/posts/2026-07-02-froude-hydraulic-jump-open-channel/) makes
at Froude number 1, between subcritical and supercritical flow.

This is the wall Fulton hit on the Seine. In a 2 m river the critical speed is 4.43 m/s, roughly
8.6 knots. Near it the wake spreads across the full channel width. The waves cannot leave the boat,
so the energy pushed into them comes straight back as resistance.

## Where this angle decides your CFD grid

In a free-surface run the domain width is the tangent of the wedge angle. The tangent of 19.47° is
0.354, so one length $L$ astern the half-width is $0.354L$. At Fr_h = 0.971 the table angle is
54.46°, and the tangent is near 1.40. The same station becomes $1.40L$, about four times wider.
Park the side wall at the deep-water habit of $0.354L$ and reflected waves mix into the hull
pressure. The resistance coefficient drifts off without a warning.

The wavelength budget moves with it. At Fr_h = 0.971 the transverse wave is 29.17 m. Forty cells
per wavelength puts that one wave past a thousand cells. The row $h = 2$ m, $V = 2$ m/s has
$\lambda = 2.56$ m and $kh = 4.9$, so the same 40-cell rule is only about a hundred cells. Same
hull, same speed, and the grid line still lengthens once depth pushes $kh$ toward critical. The
deep-water 19.47° does not depend on speed, hull form or viscosity. A narrow angle means the scheme
smeared the waves. A wide one means dispersion error got in.

## Close the domain before critical and the wake hits the wall

Fulton had no dispersion relation. The tension gauge in Bossut's tank pointed at $V = \sqrt{gh}$
first. In 2 m of water that speed is 4.43 m/s, about 8.6 knots. Once Fr_h passes 0.79 the wedge
leaves 19.47° and reaches 89.08° at 1.000. If the waves cannot leave the hull, the energy put into
them comes back as resistance.

The first question about hull resistance is not the speed itself. It is whether this depth is deep
for the wavelength that hull makes. At $h = 2$ m and $\lambda = 2.56$ m, $kh = 4.9$ and the angle
is still 19.47°. On the same depth, once the wavelength stretches to 29.17 m the wedge spreads
across the channel. Closing the side wall from the speed number alone is the same mistake as
sorting a flow by viscosity alone in
[where the Reynolds number splits regimes](/en/posts/2026-04-21-reynolds-number-scale-fluid-mechanics/).
