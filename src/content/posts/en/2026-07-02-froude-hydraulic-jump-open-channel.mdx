Turn on the faucet and let water fall onto the flat bottom of your sink. A thin, fast sheet spreads out from the point of impact, and then at some radius the water suddenly piles up into a thick ring. That ring is a **hydraulic jump**. This everyday kitchen event, remarkably, obeys the same equations as a shock wave born in a supersonic nozzle. This post walks through the Froude number that governs open-channel flow, why the water depth can leap in an instant, and how shallow water mimics a compressible gas — using a little Python and two simulations. By the end you'll see why the ring in your sink and the shock in a de Laval nozzle are cousins.

One value of $E$ maps to two depths: the upper branch (deep, slow flow) and the lower branch (shallow, fast flow). Pass the nose at the critical depth $h_c$ and the two worlds split apart.

## Flow That Doesn't Fill a Pipe

An open channel is a waterway with its top open to the atmosphere. Rivers, drainage ditches, and irrigation canals all qualify. Water filling a closed pipe is pushed by a pressure difference between inlet and outlet. Open-channel water is different. Bed slope and gravity drive the flow.

The key is that the free surface can rise and fall. Waves propagate along that surface. In shallow water, the speed of a small surface wave depends on depth alone.

$$
c = \sqrt{g h}
$$

Here $c$ is the shallow-water wave speed, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $h$ is the flow depth. Deeper water carries faster waves.

## Specific Energy and Critical Depth

The energy of the flow measured relative to the bed, expressed as a head (energy per unit weight, in length units), is the **specific energy**. In a rectangular channel of constant width $b$, writing the discharge per unit width as $q = Q/b$,

$$
E = h + \frac{u^2}{2g} = h + \frac{q^2}{2 g h^2}
$$

The first term $h$ is potential energy (depth); the second is kinetic energy. Fix $q$ and plot $E$ as a function of $h$, and an interesting curve emerges. Make the depth too shallow and velocity blows up the kinetic term; make it too deep and the potential term dominates. Somewhere in between, $E$ reaches a minimum.

Solving $dE/dh = 0$ gives the depth at that point.

$$
h_c = \left( \frac{q^2}{g} \right)^{1/3}
$$

This $h_c$ is the **critical depth**. There, $E_{\min} = \tfrac{3}{2} h_c$, and the velocity exactly equals the wave speed: $u_c = \sqrt{g h_c}$.

## The Froude Number: Tranquil and Rapid

The ratio of flow speed to wave speed sets the character of the flow. That dimensionless group is the **Froude number (ratio of inertia to gravity-wave speed)**.

$$
Fr = \frac{u}{\sqrt{g h}}
$$

When $Fr < 1$, the flow is slower than its waves. A surface disturbance from a downstream obstacle can travel back upstream. This deep, tranquil flow is called **subcritical**. When $Fr > 1$, the flow outruns its waves. Downstream signals never reach upstream. This shallow, fast flow is **supercritical**. $Fr = 1$ is the critical state, and there the depth is exactly $h_c$.

You can feel where this is going. The Froude number plays the very same role as the Mach number (ratio of flow speed to sound speed) in gas dynamics. Just as sound carries information in a compressible gas, shallow-water waves carry it in an open channel. Supercritical is supersonic; subcritical is subsonic. Inside a nozzle that reversal is the case where [the duct must widen before the flow speeds up](/en/posts/2026-05-12-laval-nozzle-supersonic-paradox/).

## The Hydraulic Jump: When Water Thickens

Trouble appears when supercritical flow must become subcritical. On the specific-energy curve there is no smooth path from the lower branch to the upper one. Nature resolves it with a discontinuity: shallow, fast water abruptly leaps to thick, slow water. That is the hydraulic jump.

The relation across the jump comes not from energy but from **momentum conservation**, because a turbulent roller inside the jump devours energy without mercy. Balancing momentum flux and hydrostatic pressure on both sides gives the conjugate-depth relation between the upstream depth $h_1$ and the downstream depth $h_2$.

$$
\frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \sqrt{1 + 8\,Fr_1^2} - 1 \right)
$$

$Fr_1$ is the Froude number just before the jump (supercritical). The specific energy lost in the jump falls out cleanly.

$$
\Delta E = \frac{(h_2 - h_1)^3}{4\, h_1 h_2}
$$

That loss is exactly why engineers deliberately create hydraulic jumps at dam spillways and stilling basins. They scatter the destructive kinetic energy of fast water into turbulence, protecting the downstream bed from erosion.



## Reproducing It in Code

Let's compute the conjugate depth and the energy loss directly. Take a unit-width discharge $q = 0.8\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$ and an upstream depth $h_1 = 0.2\ \mathrm{m}$.

```python
import numpy as np

g = 9.81  # m/s^2

def froude(u, h):
    """Froude number = flow speed / shallow-water wave speed."""
    return u / np.sqrt(g * h)

def critical_depth(q):
    """Critical depth for unit-width discharge q (minimum specific energy)."""
    return (q**2 / g) ** (1.0 / 3.0)

def specific_energy(h, q):
    """Specific energy E = h + q^2 / (2 g h^2)."""
    return h + q**2 / (2.0 * g * h**2)

def conjugate_depth(h1, q):
    """Downstream depth h2 for an upstream (supercritical) depth h1."""
    u1 = q / h1
    fr1 = froude(u1, h1)
    return 0.5 * h1 * (np.sqrt(1.0 + 8.0 * fr1**2) - 1.0)

def jump_dissipation(h1, h2):
    """Specific energy lost in the jump (turbulent dissipation)."""
    return (h2 - h1)**3 / (4.0 * h1 * h2)

q, h1 = 0.8, 0.2
u1 = q / h1
h2 = conjugate_depth(h1, q)
u2 = q / h2

print(f"upstream (super)  u1={u1:.2f} m/s  Fr1={froude(u1,h1):.2f}")
print(f"after jump        h2={h2:.3f} m  u2={u2:.2f} m/s  Fr2={froude(u2,h2):.2f}")
print(f"critical depth    hc={critical_depth(q):.3f} m")
print(f"energy loss       dE={jump_dissipation(h1,h2):.3f} m")
```

The output:

```text
upstream (super)  u1=4.00 m/s  Fr1=2.86
after jump        h2=0.714 m  u2=1.12 m/s  Fr2=0.42
critical depth    hc=0.403 m
energy loss       dE=0.238 m
```

A supercritical flow at $Fr_1 = 2.86$ became a subcritical flow at $Fr_2 = 0.42$. The depth thickened by a factor of 3.6 and the velocity slowed by the same factor (continuity, $u h = \text{const}$). The price is 0.238 m of specific energy scattered into turbulence. That the downstream depth $h_2 = 0.714$ m exceeds the critical depth $h_c = 0.403$ m confirms the flow is now subcritical.

Read the printed numbers as specific energy and the jump closes. Upstream speed 4.00 m/s is discharge 0.8 over depth 0.2. The square root of 9.81 times 0.2 is about 1.40 m/s, and 4.00 over 1.40 is the printed Fr 2.86, so a surface wave cannot run upstream. Specific energy is depth plus speed squared over 2g: about 1.016 m upstream and 0.778 m downstream. The gap is the printed loss, 0.238 m, roughly a quarter of the upstream head. Critical depth 0.403 m sits between 0.2 and 0.714, so the jump crosses the nose. Minimum energy is 1.5 times 0.403, about 0.605 m, and 0.778 m is still above it.

Fr equal to 1 in the conjugate relation gives a depth ratio of 1, no jump. The printed Fr 2.86 gives about 3.57, and times 0.2 m is 0.714 m. Fr 5 is 1 plus 200, square root about 14.2, and half of that minus 1 is about 6.6. Same relation as the line that says the ratio passes six.

## When Shallow Water Impersonates a Gas

Here is the highlight. The steady continuity equation for shallow water in a channel of constant width is $u h = \text{const}$. The steady continuity equation for a compressible gas in a duct of constant area is $\rho v = \text{const}$. Depth $h$ maps exactly onto gas density $\rho$.

Differentiate the shallow-water energy equation $h + u^2/(2g) = \text{const}$ to get $u\,du + g\,dh = 0$, insert the wave speed $u_c = \sqrt{g h}$, and you find that shallow-water waves occupy the same seat as acoustic waves in a gas. Collecting it all yields this analogy.

| Shallow water | Compressible gas |
|---|---|
| depth $h$ | density $\rho$ |
| wave speed $\sqrt{gh}$ | sound speed $\sqrt{\kappa R T}$ |
| Froude number $Fr$ | Mach number $M$ |
| supercritical / subcritical | supersonic / subsonic |
| hydraulic jump | shock wave |

This analogy corresponds to a fictitious gas with $\kappa = 2$ ($h \leftrightarrow T$, $h^2 \leftrightarrow p$). That is why early aeronautical researchers, instead of expensive supersonic wind tunnels, placed objects in a "shallow water table" and studied shock patterns qualitatively by watching the surface jumps. The ring in your sink resembles a bow shock ahead of a two-dimensional body for exactly this reason.

## Matching the jump height with energy misses the downstream depth

**The Froude number $Fr = u/\sqrt{gh}$** is the Mach number of open-channel flow. $Fr<1$ is subcritical, $Fr>1$ is supercritical, $Fr=1$ is critical. Fit the jump height by conserving specific energy and the downstream state stays on the upstream branch. The printed 0.714 m comes from momentum. Swap the conjugate depth $h_2/h_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}(\sqrt{1+8Fr_1^2}-1)$ for an energy statement and that depth does not come back.

The same Fr splits a free surface in another place: a wake. The reason a speed increase can leave the wedge angle stuck is [where depth Froude opens the Kelvin angle](/en/posts/2026-09-24-kelvin-wake-angle-depth-froude/). Jump Fr and wake Fr both measure speed by wave speed. One sets a depth ratio. The other pins an angle.
