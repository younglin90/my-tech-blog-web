What sets the threshold? Reynolds answered this in 1883 with a single dye streak in Manchester, and his answer compresses into one dimensionless number. The note stays with why the threshold sits near Re ≈ 2300, and how the boundary layer past it splits into a viscous sublayer, a buffer, and a log region. ## When the colored streak suddenly tangles

Reynolds injected dye through the centerline of a glass pipe. He varied diameter $d$, mean speed $u$, and kinematic viscosity $\nu$, asking one thing: when does the streak fall apart?

Across every combination, transition started near a single value of one dimensionless group:

$$
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{u\, d}{\nu}
$$

Here $u$ is the mean velocity, $d$ is the characteristic length (pipe diameter), and $\nu = \mu / \rho$ is the kinematic viscosity (dynamic viscosity over density). Cross the threshold and the streak loses its balance.

## What Re ≈ 2300 actually says

Re is more than an experimental constant. Non-dimensionalize the momentum equation and the viscous term gets multiplied by exactly $1/\mathrm{Re}$. So Re is the ratio of **inertial to viscous forces**.

- Low Re → viscosity dominates, small disturbances are smeared out → laminar flow survives.
- High Re → inertia dominates, small disturbances grow nonlinearly → flow tips into turbulence.

For pipe flow the empirical critical Re sits around 2300. The number is fragile, though. With a polished pipe, vibration-free mounts, and a smooth inlet, researchers have held laminar flow up to Re ≈ 50,000. So "Re_crit ≈ 2300" is a working engineering threshold; the real story is whether disturbances have an **energy pathway** wide enough to amplify. The same number splitting an insect's swim from a ship's wake is the subject of [how Reynolds number changes the scale of a flow](/en/posts/2026-04-21-reynolds-number-scale-fluid-mechanics/).

For a flat-plate boundary layer (the thin viscous-dominated film near a wall), the relevant scale is different. With distance $x$ from the leading edge, $\mathrm{Re}_x = U_\infty x / \nu$ goes critical near $3 \times 10^5$. Use boundary-layer thickness $\delta$ and the threshold becomes $\mathrm{Re}_\delta \approx 2700$. The same physics keeps reappearing under different scaling.

## Prandtl's boundary layer — outer and inner

A year after Reynolds' transition law (1904), Prandtl asked a different question. If viscosity is small, why does friction near walls still matter at all?

His answer: separate the region where viscosity matters from the rest. The thin film where the no-slip condition forces the fluid from zero up to free-stream speed is the **boundary layer**; outside it, treat the flow as inviscid potential flow. With this split, the 2.5–25 mm sliver above an aircraft wing is enough to capture viscous drag, transition, and separation.

A turbulent boundary layer is not one slab. Once distance from the wall is non-dimensionalized correctly, a universal layered structure appears:

$$
y^+ = \frac{y\, u_\tau}{\nu}, \qquad u_\tau = \sqrt{\tau_w / \rho}
$$

$u_\tau$ is the **wall friction velocity** (square root of wall shear stress over density). $\nu / u_\tau$ is the distance over which molecular momentum diffusion acts — the **viscous length**. So $y^+$ is "distance from the wall, measured in viscous lengths."

## Law of the wall — three regions

In a flat-plate turbulent boundary layer, scaling velocity by $u_\tau$ as $u^+ = u / u_\tau$ exposes three regions:

| Region | Range | Profile |
|--------|-------|---------|
| Viscous sublayer | $0 < y^+ < 5$ | $u^+ = y^+$ |
| Buffer layer | $5 < y^+ < 30$ | smooth blend |
| Log-law (inertial) | $30 < y^+ < 0.2\, \delta^+$ | $u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa} \ln y^+ + B$ |

Here $\kappa \approx 0.41$ is the von Kármán constant and $B \approx 5.0$ for a smooth wall.

Right next to the wall, eddies are choked off; molecular diffusion handles momentum transport. With shear stress nearly constant, integrating $\tau_w = \mu\, du/dy$ recovers $u^+ = y^+$ exactly. As a scale: at 20 m/s, 2 m downstream of a leading edge, $y^+ = 1$ corresponds to roughly 0.02 mm — the viscous sublayer is about a fifth of a hair's width.

The log region falls out of Prandtl's **mixing length** argument (an eddy's average travel before it equilibrates with its surroundings). Set $\ell = \kappa y$ near the wall, assume constant shear stress, integrate, and the logarithmic profile drops into your lap.

Move $B$ from 5.0 up to 5.5 and the log line shifts upward in parallel — rough walls reduce $B$, suction increases it. The roughness state of an entire surface compresses into the intercept of one straight line.

Drop only the log law in at y+ = 30 and u+ is (1/0.41) ln(30) + 5.0. ln(30) is about 3.40; divide by 0.41 and you get about 8.30; add B and the result is about 13.3. The linear law at that same height says u+ = 30, so the two disagree by nearly 17. At y+ = 5 the linear law is 5. Force the log law there and ln(5) ≈ 1.61 gives u+ ≈ 8.9. The gap between 5 and 8.9 is the buffer. Stitch the formulas into one expression and that band gets counted twice.

At 20 m/s and 2 m from the leading edge, y+ = 1 was 0.02 mm, so y+ = 5 is 0.10 mm. The linear law lives inside that tenth of a millimeter. Across the wall-function decade from 30 to 300, a tenfold rise in y+ adds about 2.30 to the log and only about 5.6 to u+ after dividing by 0.41. Sliding B from 5.0 to 5.5 lifts the whole log layer by 0.5. That 0.5 is about 9 percent of the 5.6 gained over one decade. Miss the intercept by half a unit because the wall is rough, and the friction velocity from the wall function shifts with it.

## Mixing length — eddies as molecules

In laminar flow, momentum is carried by **molecules**. Newton's viscosity law $\tau = \mu\, du/dy$ is the result. In turbulent flow, eddies (organized fluid blobs of many sizes) take that role.

Prandtl borrowed the idea of a mean free path from kinetic gas theory. Call the average distance an eddy travels before merging with its surroundings the **mixing length** $\ell$. Then the velocity fluctuation $u'$ is roughly the velocity difference over that distance, and the Reynolds shear stress becomes

$$
\overline{u' v'} \approx -\ell^2 \left(\frac{d\bar{u}}{dy}\right)^2
$$

That defines an **eddy viscosity** $\nu_t$:

$$
\nu_t = \ell^2 \left|\frac{d\bar{u}}{dy}\right|
$$

Near a wall, $\nu_t$ can exceed molecular $\nu$ by four orders of magnitude. Air's molecular kinematic viscosity is $\nu \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ m²/s, while atmospheric eddy viscosity reaches 0.01–0.1 m²/s near the surface and 1–100 m²/s in large-scale flows.

The mixing-length idea is a hypothesis, but its scaffolding survives in zeroth-order RANS, in $k$-$\varepsilon$, and in $k$-$\omega$. Treating eddies like molecules turned out to be the simplification that built industrial CFD.

## Laminar vs turbulent profile in NumPy

Pipe laminar flow has a clean closed form — the Hagen–Poiseuille parabola. Turbulent flow in the same pipe is much flatter; a 1/n power law approximates it well.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def laminar_poiseuille(r, R, U_max):
    """Hagen-Poiseuille: paraboloid-of-revolution profile."""
    return U_max * (1.0 - (r / R) ** 2)

def turbulent_power_law(r, R, U_centerline, n=7):
    """1/n power-law turbulent fit (n=7 holds near Re ~ 1e5)."""
    return U_centerline * (1.0 - np.abs(r) / R) ** (1.0 / n)

def law_of_the_wall(y_plus, kappa=0.41, B=5.0):
    """Three-region composite: viscous / buffer blend / log."""
    y_plus = np.asarray(y_plus, dtype=float)
    u_visc = y_plus
    u_log = (1.0 / kappa) * np.log(np.maximum(y_plus, 1e-12)) + B
    in_buffer = (y_plus > 5) & (y_plus < 30)
    t = np.clip((y_plus - 5) / 25.0, 0.0, 1.0)
    u_blend = u_visc * (1 - t) + u_log * t
    return np.where(y_plus <= 5, u_visc,
           np.where(y_plus >= 30, u_log, u_blend))

R = 1.0
r = np.linspace(-R, R, 201)
u_lam = laminar_poiseuille(r, R, U_max=1.0)
u_tur = turbulent_power_law(r, R, U_centerline=1.0, n=7)

bulk_lam = np.trapz(u_lam * np.abs(r), r) / np.trapz(np.abs(r), r)
bulk_tur = np.trapz(u_tur * np.abs(r), r) / np.trapz(np.abs(r), r)
print(f"laminar  bulk/centerline = {bulk_lam:.3f}")
print(f"turbulent bulk/centerline = {bulk_tur:.3f}")
# laminar  bulk/centerline = 0.500
# turbulent bulk/centerline = 0.817

y_plus = np.logspace(-1, 3, 200)
u_plus = law_of_the_wall(y_plus)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
axes[0].plot(r, u_lam, label="laminar")
axes[0].plot(r, u_tur, label="turbulent (n=7)")
axes[0].set(xlabel="r/R", ylabel="u / U_max", title="pipe profile")
axes[0].legend()
axes[1].semilogx(y_plus, u_plus)
axes[1].set(xlabel="y+", ylabel="u+", title="law of the wall")
plt.tight_layout()
```

Two numbers carry the punchline. **Laminar** mean velocity is exactly half the centerline value — the volume of a paraboloid versus its bounding cylinder. **Turbulent** mean is 81% of centerline. The same bulk flow needs a lower peak because turbulence **mixes momentum across the cross-section**. That uniformity is the visible face of an eddy viscosity that sits four orders of magnitude above the molecular value.

## Why transition matters in practice

The split between laminar and turbulent is not just a curiosity.

- Aircraft wings designed for **natural laminar flow (NLF)** can cut friction drag by nearly half by extending the laminar region.
- Heat exchangers deliberately **trip turbulence** to raise the heat-transfer coefficient. The same momentum mixing carries heat.
- Pipeline pumping costs jump near the critical Re. Avoiding the transition window often dictates the operating point.
- In LES and RANS, the first off-wall cell must land at $y^+ \approx 1$ to resolve the viscous sublayer, or in $30 < y^+ < 300$ if a wall function will fill in. A single one-digit number sets the grid budget.

## Put the first point in the log layer and the sublayer drops out

If the first cell sits near y+ of 30, the band where u+ equals y+ is never computed. Wall shear then hangs on the log-law intercept B. For a smooth wall B is 5.0. Leave that number on a rough wall and the 0.5 parallel shift above moves into the friction velocity. The code's 0.817 is the smooth-pipe 1/7-power fit near Re of 100,000. At the same centerline speed the turbulent volume flux is 1.63 times the laminar 0.500. Attach that ratio to a pipe with the wrong intercept and the flow rate is off.

The ratio holds only after the profile is fully developed. How far a pipe must run before the parabola, or the 1/7 power, even exists is counted in [entrance length and Hagen–Poiseuille](/en/posts/2026-07-23-pipe-entrance-hagen-poiseuille/). Skip that length and the 0.500 versus 0.817 comparison gets applied to a station that does not have either profile yet.
