## How much does pressure rise where the pipe area doubles?

A small pipe opens abruptly into a larger one. The area doubles, so the velocity halves. Bernoulli says the pressure rises by $\tfrac12\rho V_1^2 \times 0.75$. Measure it and you get something near $0.5$. Bernoulli is off by a factor of 1.5.

The people who first wrote this gap down were French military engineers of the 18th century. This post follows their result, the Borda–Carnot relation. The derivation uses momentum instead of energy. Carnot's "lost velocity" theorem explains where the loss goes. At the end we solve a duct where two streams mix and check the numbers. You will come away knowing when the loss coefficient $K=(1-\sigma)^2$ holds and when it does not.

## The lost velocity, seen by a revolutionary-era engineer

Lazare Carnot entered the Royal Engineering School at Mézières in 1771. His teacher was Gaspard Monge, who had taken over the mathematics chair from Bossut. Bossut later measured fluid resistance together with d'Alembert. Borda, before Bossut, had studied Newton's theory of fluid resistance in depth.

In 1784 Carnot published a paper on machines. In it he introduced the idea of an "energy loss" in inelastic collisions, an unfamiliar notion at the time. When two bodies collide and stick, momentum survives but kinetic energy (vis viva, the "living force") drops. Carnot counted the drop exactly. The loss is the sum of the kinetic energies of the velocities each body **loses** in the impact.

Carnot then applied the same idea to pipe flow. When the cross-section widens or narrows abruptly, Bernoulli's theorem fails. A head loss (the energy the flow loses, expressed as a height) appears. That relation is what we now call the Borda–Carnot equation. Carnot later reorganized the army on the revolutionary Committee of Public Safety. His son was Sadi Carnot of thermodynamics.

## Derivation: one assumption about the pressure on the step face

Take a control volume (an imaginary box for mass and momentum bookkeeping) from just after the step to where the flow is uniform again. Neglect wall friction. Mass conservation is simple.

$$V_2 = \sigma V_1, \qquad \sigma = \frac{A_1}{A_2}$$

$V_1, V_2$ are the mean velocities in the small and large pipes, and $\sigma$ is the area ratio.

There is one key assumption. The pressure on the step face (the annular wall of area $A_2-A_1$) equals the jet pressure $p_1$. The eddy region behind the step is nearly stagnant, and the jet passes straight beside it. So the jet pressure is transmitted to the stagnant fluid. Under this assumption the axial momentum balance is:

$$p_1 A_1 + p_1 (A_2 - A_1) - p_2 A_2 = \rho V_2^2 A_2 - \rho V_1^2 A_1$$

The left side is the net pressure force on the inlet, step face and outlet. The right side is outgoing minus incoming momentum flux. Rearranging gives:

$$p_2 - p_1 = \rho V_2 (V_1 - V_2) = \rho V_1^2\,\sigma(1-\sigma)$$

Bernoulli instead gives $p_2-p_1=\tfrac12\rho V_1^2(1-\sigma^2)$. With $\sigma=0.5$, momentum gives $0.25\rho V_1^2$ and Bernoulli gives $0.375\rho V_1^2$. That ratio is the 1.5× from the opening. Converting the difference to energy per unit mass gives the loss.

$$h_L = \frac{(V_1 - V_2)^2}{2g} = (1-\sigma)^2 \frac{V_1^2}{2g}$$

$h_L$ is the head loss and $g$ is gravity. The loss coefficient $K=(1-\sigma)^2$ reaches 1 in the limit of a pipe discharging into a large tank ($\sigma\to0$). All of the jet's kinetic energy is gone.

## The loss is the kinetic energy of the lost velocity

Look again at the numerator $(V_1-V_2)^2$. A fluid particle enters at $V_1$ and leaves at $V_2$. The velocity it lost is $V_1-V_2$. The loss is exactly the kinetic energy of moving at that lost velocity. That is the same form as Carnot's collision theorem.

Let a mass $m_1$ at speed $V_1$ hit a mass $m_2$ at rest and stick. The common speed afterward is $V=m_1V_1/(m_1+m_2)$. The loss is:

$$\Delta E = \tfrac12 m_1 (V_1 - V)^2 + \tfrac12 m_2 (0 - V)^2$$

Each term is the kinetic energy of the velocity that body lost (or gained). In a sudden expansion, the stagnant fluid behind the step plays the role of $m_2$. But that fluid is not carried downstream. It contributes nothing to the mass flow, so the second term drops out.

The view extends directly to two mixing streams. When stream tubes (thin tubes bounded by streamlines) with different speeds mix in one duct, the loss is:

$$\Delta e = \frac{1}{Q}\sum_k \tfrac12 (u_k - V_2)^2\, \Delta\psi_k$$

$Q$ is the total flow rate, $\Delta\psi_k$ is the flow rate carried by tube $k$, and $u_k$ is its inlet speed. The details of mixing (turbulent or laminar diffusion) never enter. Once mass and momentum balance, the loss is fixed. As shown in the earlier post [Bernoulli's constant differs across streamlines](/en/posts/2026-09-03-bernoulli-constant-across-streamlines/), the fact that each stream tube carries its own Bernoulli constant matters here.

## Code: a mixing duct with 80 stream tubes

The real eddy behind a step needs a 2D solver. Here we solve a simpler problem with the same physics. A constant-area duct receives a central jet (width $\sigma$, speed 1) and a slow outer stream (speed $\varepsilon$). The walls are slip walls. The two streams mix through viscosity $\nu$.

In the boundary-layer approximation (a parabolic model that drops streamwise diffusion), written in von Mises coordinates (streamfunction $\psi$ as the cross-stream coordinate):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \nu\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \psi^2}\!\left(\frac{u^2}{2}\right) - \frac{1}{\rho u}\frac{dp}{dx}$$

Each cell is one stream tube carrying the same flow rate. At every step, Newton's method sets $dp/dx$ so that the summed tube widths $\sum \Delta\psi/u$ equal the duct width. Written this way, the discrete scheme conserves momentum exactly. The limit $\varepsilon\to0$ is the stagnant-fluid limit of Borda–Carnot.

```python
import numpy as np

def stream_tubes(sigma, eps, n=40):
    # n stream tubes (equal flux dpsi): jet in the middle (u=1), slow stream outside (u=eps)
    Q = sigma + eps * (1 - sigma)
    dpsi = Q / n
    n_jet = round(n * sigma / Q)
    k = np.arange(n)
    jet = np.abs(k - (n - 1) / 2) < n_jet / 2
    u = np.where(jet, 1.0, eps)
    return u, dpsi, jet

def mix_step(u, P, dpsi, nu, H, dx):
    q = u**2 / 2
    qg = np.concatenate(([q[0]], q, [q[-1]]))          # slip walls: dq/dpsi = 0
    Dq = nu * (qg[2:] - 2 * q + qg[:-2]) / dpsi**2     # (1/u) * viscous term
    for _ in range(20):                                 # dp/dx fixed by duct width (Newton)
        un = u + dx * (Dq - P / u)
        if un.min() <= 0:
            return None, P                              # a slow tube stopped -> reversal
        f = np.sum(dpsi / un) - H
        df = np.sum(dpsi * dx / (u * un**2))
        P -= f / df
        if abs(f) < 1e-13:
            break
    return u + dx * (Dq - P / u), P

def mixing_duct(sigma, eps, nu, n=40, tol=1e-3):
    u, dpsi, jet = stream_tubes(sigma, eps, n)
    u0 = u.copy()
    H = np.sum(dpsi / u)                    # duct width (~1)
    Q = n * dpsi                            # equal flux per tube
    V2 = Q / H
    M0 = np.sum(u * dpsi) / H               # inlet momentum flux / H
    dp_mom = M0 - V2**2                     # pressure rise from momentum balance
    E0 = np.sum(u**2 / 2 * dpsi) / Q
    x, p, P, x99 = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, None
    while np.abs(u - V2).max() > tol:
        dx = 0.4 * dpsi**2 / (nu * u.max())
        un, P = mix_step(u, P, dpsi, nu, H, dx)
        if un is None:
            return dict(stall=True, x=x, dp=p, dp_mom=dp_mom)
        u, x, p = un, x + dx, p + P * dx
        if x99 is None and p >= 0.99 * dp_mom:
            x99 = x
    loss = E0 - (p + np.sum(u**2 / 2 * dpsi) / Q)
    # Carnot's theorem: sum of KE of each tube's lost (gained) velocity
    carnot = np.sum(0.5 * (u0 - V2)**2 * dpsi) / Q
    return dict(stall=False, x99=x99, V2=V2, dp=p, dp_mom=dp_mom,
                dp_bern=0.5 * (1 - V2**2), loss=loss, carnot=carnot)

print("Borda-Carnot limit (eps=0, sigma=0.5): dp = %.4f, loss = %.4f, Bernoulli dp = %.4f"
      % (0.5 * 0.5, 0.5 * 0.5**2, 0.5 * (1 - 0.5**2)))
print(" eps    nu     V2    dp_num  dp_mom  dp_Bern  loss    Carnot   x99")
for eps, nu in [(0.8, 0.004), (0.6, 0.004), (0.4, 0.004), (0.2, 0.004),
                (0.1, 0.004), (0.05, 0.004), (0.2, 0.002), (0.2, 0.008)]:
    r = mixing_duct(0.5, eps, nu, n=80)
    if r["stall"]:
        print(f"{eps:4.2f} {nu:6.3f}  STALL at x={r['x']:.2f}  "
              f"dp={r['dp']:.4f} of {r['dp_mom']:.4f}")
        continue
    print(f"{eps:4.2f} {nu:6.3f} {r['V2']:6.3f} {r['dp']:7.4f} {r['dp_mom']:7.4f} "
          f"{r['dp_bern']:7.4f} {r['loss']:7.4f} {r['carnot']:7.4f} {r['x99']:6.1f}")
```

## 0.1599 versus 0.3270, and what ν could not change

Three things stand out in the table.

First, `dp_num` and `dp_mom` agree to four decimals in every row. The solver marched the mixing step by step. Yet the answer was already fixed by the difference between inlet and outlet momentum. The pressure rise is a consequence of the momentum balance.

Second, `loss` and `Carnot` also agree in every row. The duct lost energy while each tube's speed moved to $V_2$. That amount matches the summed kinetic energy of the lost velocities exactly. It is the quantity Carnot counted in collisions.

Third, $\nu$ was varied fourfold, from 0.002 to 0.008. `x99` (the distance to reach 99% of the final pressure) dropped from 16.2 to 4.1. `dp_num` and `loss` stayed at 0.1599 and 0.0831. Viscosity decides **where** the loss happens. It does not decide **how much**.

Bernoulli along the jet streamline gives 0.3270 in that row, 2.0× the real value. At $\varepsilon=0.8$ the gap is 0.0960 versus 0.0100, a factor of 9.6. The smaller the speed difference, the smaller the true pressure rise, and Bernoulli cannot follow it. As $\varepsilon$ decreases, `dp_num` approaches 0.2255 at $\varepsilon=0.05$, heading toward the Borda–Carnot limit of 0.25.

At $\varepsilon=0.03$ and below, the march stops. The adverse pressure gradient brings the slow fluid to rest. A parabolic march cannot represent reversed flow. In a real sudden expansion, this is exactly where the recirculation zone forms. Borda's assumption that the step-face pressure equals $p_1$ replaces that zone with stagnant fluid.

## Borda–Carnot today: from piping K-values to 1D codes

The sudden-expansion loss coefficient $K=(1-A_1/A_2)^2$ in piping handbooks is this relation, unchanged. It agrees well with turbulent experiments, because the recirculation behind a step behaves close to stagnant fluid. Pipe-network solvers, 1D thermal-hydraulic system codes and fuel-system models still use it as the default.

The failure cases are just as clear. First, if the inlet velocity profile is not uniform, the momentum flux is not $\rho V^2 A$. For fully developed laminar pipe flow, the momentum correction factor is $\beta=4/3$ and the energy correction factor is $\alpha=2$. The $\alpha$ from [pipe velocity profiles and the kinetic energy coefficient](/en/posts/2026-09-17-kinetic-energy-coefficient-pipe-profile/) enters the formula directly. Second, at low Reynolds numbers the recirculation zone grows long and wall friction mixes in. Third, in a gradual diffuser, separation is delayed and $K$ is much smaller.

CFD hits the same question. Quasi-1D pipe codes discretize the area-change term $p\,dA/dx$ separately. When the area jumps inside one cell, the choice of that $p$ is Borda's assumption. Use the upstream pressure and you get the Borda–Carnot loss. Treat it like a smooth nozzle and you get the lossless Bernoulli answer. Mixing tubes in ejectors (devices that use a fast jet to entrain a slow stream) and jet pumps are designed with exactly the momentum balance in the code above.

## Two conditions to check before pulling K from a table

Before using $K=(1-\sigma)^2$, check two things. Is the inlet profile nearly uniform (for fully developed turbulence, $\beta\approx1.02$ is fine)? Is the Reynolds number high enough for a recirculation zone that acts as stagnant fluid behind the step? If both hold, the pressure rise is $\rho V_2(V_1-V_2)$ and the loss is the kinetic energy of the lost velocity. You do not need to ask how the streams mix.
