## A 574x Faster Search Lost 104 Contacts

When aluminum particle agglomeration is solved with DEM, neighbor search is the first thing that slows down.
I packed 2400 grains into one cluster and looked for contact pairs.
Brute force takes 2,878,800 distance checks and finds 1539 contacts.
Cutting sectors to a quarter of the grain diameter, then scanning only 27 cells, dropped that to 5015 checks.
That is 574x fewer. The search found 1435 contacts.

104 pairs disappeared quietly. No error, no warning.
One agglomerate simply split into two.
This post traces where those 104 pairs went and how finely a sector grid can actually be cut.

## The Cost of DEM Lives in a Double Loop, Not in Communication

DPM (Discrete Parcel Method) couples particles to the flow field in both directions and nothing else.
Grains never see each other. Cost scales with grain count.

DEM (Discrete Element Method) adds collisions, repulsion and attraction between grains.
The structure is nearly identical to molecular dynamics.
Because grain $i$ must know who it touches, a pairwise loop appears.

$$
N_{\text{pair}} = \frac{N(N-1)}{2}
$$

$N$ is the grain count. 2400 grains give 2.88 million pairs; 9310 grains give 43.3 million.
The wall is this quadratic term, not MPI traffic.
Even with good parallelization, each rank runs the same double loop locally.
Escaping into more cores leads straight into the [weak scaling trap](/en/posts/2026-10-04-weak-scaling-krylov-grid-levels/).

## What a Sector Grid Removes Is Distance Evaluations

Cut the domain into cubic sectors of edge $s$.
Register each grain in its own sector list and restrict the search to that sector and its neighbors.
Scanning $2k+1$ cells along each axis sweeps the volume

$$
V_k = \big[(2k+1)\,s\big]^3
$$

where $k$ is the shell radius, the number of layers you look through.
The expected number of grains inside is $N V_k / L^3$, with $L$ the domain edge.
Distance evaluations drop to a term linear in $N$.

## There Is Exactly One Safety Condition

Grains $i$ and $j$ touch when their center distance falls below the sum of radii.

$$
|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j| < r_i + r_j \le 2\,r_{\max} = d_{\max}
$$

Here $r_{\max}$ is the largest radius in the domain and $d_{\max}$ the matching diameter.
A $2k+1$ stencil, meanwhile, guarantees a search reach of $k\,s$.
A grain can sit flush against its own sector boundary, so the reach you can rely on is $k\,s$, no more.
The condition for missing nothing is therefore a single line.

$$
k\,s \ge d_{\max} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k \ge \left\lceil \frac{d_{\max}}{s} \right\rceil
$$

With $s = d_{\max}$ you need $k=1$, so 27 cells suffice.
Cutting down to $s = d_{\max}/4$ demands $k=4$, which is 729 cells.
The experiment above kept 27 cells while shrinking $s$ by four, so the condition broke.
Those 104 pairs were touching outside the stencil.

## Six Faces, Twelve Edges, Eight Corners

A $k=1$ stencil is one home sector plus 26 neighbors.
Those 26 split into 6 that share a face, 12 that share an edge, and 8 that share only a corner.
The slide deck I reviewed listed 12 faces and 6 edges.
The two numbers are swapped.

The distinction matters because the 8 corner neighbors are the easiest to drop.
Faces and edges alone give 19 cells. Running the same cluster with 19 cells missed 1 pair out of 1539.
One pair looks harmless, but if it was the link holding an agglomerate together, one cluster gets reported as two.
A corner neighbor only fires when both grains sit near the shared corner, so test cases almost never hit it.
It hits halfway through a production run instead.

## One Oversized Grain Sets the Grid for the Whole Domain

What enters the inequality is $d_{\max}$, not the mean diameter.
For monodisperse grains that makes no difference. Agglomeration problems are rarely monodisperse.

Idea 1 in the reviewed deck states as assumption 1 that the center grain has the largest diameter in the domain.
The center grain attracts everything else, and the rest only feel repulsion among themselves.
In that setup a single grain fixes $d_{\max}$.

In the experiment the center diameter was 0.060 and the largest of the rest was 0.030.
2399 grains sit at or below 0.030, yet the sector edge has to be 0.060 to stay safe.
Every sector then holds eight times more volume than it needs.
Distance checks scale with it: 269,450 as measured.

## Cut Finer and Empty-Cell Lookups Become the New Cost

So why not shrink $s$ and raise $k$?
Substituting $s = d_{\max}/k$ turns the swept volume into

$$
V_k = \left[\frac{(2k+1)d_{\max}}{k}\right]^3 = \left(2 + \frac{1}{k}\right)^3 d_{\max}^3
$$

which is $27\,d_{\max}^3$ at $k=1$ and converges to $8\,d_{\max}^3$ as $k\to\infty$.
However fine you cut, distance checks only ever fall by a factor of 27/8, that is 3.4x.
The number of sectors to visit, meanwhile, grows as $(2k+1)^3 \approx 8k^3$.
At $k=4$ that is 729 lookups per grain, so 1.75 million hash lookups for 2400 grains.
Most of them hit empty sectors.

The measurement says exactly that. $k=4$ with $s=d_{\max}/4$ cut distance checks to 125,548,
but sector lookups rose to 1,749,600 and the wall clock came out at 0.291 s.
That is 3.5x slower than 0.082 s for $k=1$ with $s=d_{\max}$.
Distance checks improved 2.1x while the total got worse.

## Five Settings on the Same Cloud of 2400 Grains

Assumptions and measurements live in one file. Standard library only.

```python
import math, random

def pack_particles(n, seed=7):
    """One largest grain at the center; the rest injected as a Gaussian cloud"""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    p = []
    for _ in range(n):
        d = 0.012 + 0.018 * rng.random() ** 2          # diameter 0.012 ~ 0.030
        c = [min(0.97, max(0.03, 0.5 + rng.gauss(0, 0.105))) for _ in range(3)]
        p.append((c[0], c[1], c[2], 0.5 * d))
    p[0] = (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.030)                      # center: diameter 0.060
    return p

def brute_contacts(p):
    pairs, ev = set(), 0
    for i in range(len(p)):
        xi, yi, zi, ri = p[i]
        for j in range(i + 1, len(p)):
            xj, yj, zj, rj = p[j]
            ev += 1
            if (xi-xj)**2 + (yi-yj)**2 + (zi-zj)**2 < (ri + rj)**2:
                pairs.add((i, j))
    return pairs, ev

def sector_bins(p, s, start=0):
    b = {}
    for i in range(start, len(p)):
        x, y, z, _ = p[i]
        b.setdefault((int(x/s), int(y/s), int(z/s)), []).append(i)
    return b

def neighbor_offsets(shell):
    r = range(-shell, shell + 1)
    return [(a, b, c) for a in r for b in r for c in r]

def binned_contacts(p, s, shell, start=0):
    bins, off = sector_bins(p, s, start), neighbor_offsets(shell)
    pairs, ev, look = set(), 0, 0
    for i in range(start, len(p)):
        xi, yi, zi, ri = p[i]
        cx, cy, cz = int(xi/s), int(yi/s), int(zi/s)
        for a, b, c in off:
            look += 1
            for j in bins.get((cx+a, cy+b, cz+c), ()):
                if j <= i:
                    continue
                xj, yj, zj, rj = p[j]
                ev += 1
                if (xi-xj)**2 + (yi-yj)**2 + (zi-zj)**2 < (ri + rj)**2:
                    pairs.add((i, j))
    return pairs, ev, look

def split_big_contacts(p, s, shell):
    """Grain 0 (the largest) is handled by brute force; sectors sized by the rest"""
    pairs, ev, look = binned_contacts(p, s, shell, start=1)
    xb, yb, zb, rb = p[0]
    for j in range(1, len(p)):
        xj, yj, zj, rj = p[j]
        ev += 1
        if (xb-xj)**2 + (yb-yj)**2 + (zb-zj)**2 < (rb + rj)**2:
            pairs.add((0, j))
    return pairs, ev, look

N = 2400
P = pack_particles(N)
DMAX = 2 * max(q[3] for q in P)                 # largest diameter overall
DSUB = 2 * max(q[3] for q in P[1:])             # largest diameter excluding the center grain
truth, ev0 = brute_contacts(P)
print("N=%d  L=1.0  d_max=%.3f  d_max(sub)=%.3f" % (N, DMAX, DSUB))
print("all-pairs : contacts=%d  dist=%d" % (len(truth), ev0))
print()
print("%-20s %-7s %-6s %-9s %-9s %-5s %s" %
      ("case", "s/dmax", "cells", "dist", "lookups", "miss", "safe?"))
for f, shell in ((1.0, 1), (0.5, 1), (0.25, 1), (0.25, 4)):
    s = DMAX * f
    got, ev, look = binned_contacts(P, s, shell)
    print("%-20s %-7.2f %-6d %-9d %-9d %-5d %s" %
          ("sector shell=%d" % shell, f, (2*shell+1)**3, ev, look,
           len(truth - got), "yes" if shell*s >= DMAX - 1e-12 else "NO"))
got, ev, look = split_big_contacts(P, DSUB, 1)
print("%-20s %-7.2f %-6d %-9d %-9d %-5d %s" %
      ("big-split shell=1", DSUB/DMAX, 27, ev, look, len(truth - got), "yes"))
print()
print("required shell = ceil(d_max/s) :", [(f, math.ceil(1/f)) for f in (1.0, 0.5, 0.25)])
```

Look at the third row. `s/dmax=0.50` with `shell=1` violates the safety condition yet misses nothing.
That is luck. Only one grain has diameter 0.060, and none of its contact partners happened to sit in a corner direction.
Reseed the cloud or advance one step and the number changes.
"I ran it and it was fine" is not evidence for a condition of this kind.
It is the same trap as [what a verification case actually measures](/en/posts/2026-10-07-sod-vortex-order-verification/).

## Splitting Off the Big Grain Buys Safety and Speed Together

The last row is the fix.
Handle the one center grain by brute force separately, and size sectors by the largest of the remaining 2399 grains, 0.030.
The extra cost is 2399 distance checks, one $O(N)$ pass.

Distance checks fall to 41,229, which is 6.5x fewer than the 269,450 of the $s=d_{\max}$ setting.
Sector lookups stay flat at 64,773.
Nothing is missed, and this time the inequality guarantees it rather than luck.
Among the remaining grains, $r_i + r_j \le 0.030 = 1 \cdot s$ holds by construction.
Wall clock is 0.023 s, one twenty-ninth of the 0.668 s brute force.

The gap widens with scale. An agglomerate is a few large grains with thousands of small ones attached.
Treating the handful in the tail of the size distribution separately needs less code than building a two-level grid.
Much like [where a flamelet library folds](/en/posts/2026-10-06-flamelet-library-slf-fpv-progress-variable/),
the point is to break the structure in which a tiny minority dictates the resolution of the whole table.

## What Those 104 Missing Contacts Change

Miss one contact and the repulsion for that pair becomes zero.
The two grains pass through each other, or stay overlapped and drop out of the surface-area update.
In aluminum particle combustion the overlapped area of an agglomerate maps directly onto oxide-layer area.
Burn rate comes out too high. Mass conservation never breaks, so no residual flags it.

So when you write a sector size into search code, three things are worth checking.
Whether $k\,s \ge d_{\max}$ holds, whether that $d_{\max}$ is remeasured every step (merging and breakup change it),
and how many grains are single-handedly pushing $d_{\max}$ up.
If the answer to the third is a single digit, those grains are cheaper counted separately than binned.
