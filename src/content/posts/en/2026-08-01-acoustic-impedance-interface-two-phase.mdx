## Add a Liquid and the Acoustic Time Step Dies First

A fully explicit compressible two-phase step is chained to the acoustic CFL limit.

$$
\Delta t \le \frac{\Delta x}{\max(|u| + c)}
$$

$c$ is the sound speed and $\Delta x$ the cell size. $c$ is different in each phase. Air is 347 m/s, water 1500 m/s. One liquid cell tightens the global step by more than a factor of four.

Material inside the liquid moves at about 1 m/s. In Mach number (flow speed over sound speed) that is $10^{-3}$. The motion worth watching is the interface deforming. The step is set by sound waves nobody is following.

The old workaround treated the liquid as incompressible. The step opens up, and the sound inside the liquid disappears with it. So does the question of how a droplet answers a wave.

This paper splits the equations into advection and acoustics. Advection stays explicit. Acoustics go implicit through a projection. What remains is this.

$$
\frac{1}{\Delta t} = \frac{1}{\Delta t_{\mathrm{conv}}} + \frac{1}{\Delta t_{\mu}} + \frac{1}{\Delta t_{\sigma}}
$$

$\Delta t_{\mathrm{conv}} = \Delta x / \max\lVert \vec{u} \rVert$ is advection, $\Delta t_{\mu} = \rho \Delta x^{2} / 2\mu$ is viscosity, and $\Delta t_{\sigma} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\max(\rho)\Delta x^{3}/\sigma}$ is surface tension. No $c$ anywhere.

## What Stays Continuous Across the Interface, and What Breaks

A sharp interface treats $\Gamma$ as a surface of zero thickness and imposes the conditions on it directly. The paper uses three.

$$
[\vec{u}]_{\Gamma} = 0, \qquad
[p]_{\Gamma} = \sigma \kappa + 2\left[\mu \frac{\partial u_n}{\partial n}\right]_{\Gamma} - \frac{2}{3}\left[\mu \nabla \cdot \vec{u}\right]_{\Gamma}, \qquad
[-k \nabla T \cdot \vec{n}]_{\Gamma} = 0
$$

$[\,\cdot\,]_{\Gamma}$ is the jump across the interface, $\sigma$ surface tension, $\kappa$ curvature, $\mu$ viscosity, $k$ thermal conductivity. Velocity is continuous, pressure jumps by the capillary part, heat flux is continuous. With no mass transfer, temperature is continuous too ($[T]_{\Gamma}=0$).

Density is not on the list. Each phase's equation of state sets its own density. Nothing averages density near the interface, so no intermediate state that neither material owns ever appears. That is where the fake sound speed, later, gets blocked.

The closure is cubic.

$$
\rho^{3} + a_{1}\rho^{2} + a_{2}\rho + a_{3} = 0
$$

The coefficients $a_1, a_2, a_3$ depend on pressure, temperature, and the material. van der Waals, Peng-Robinson, and Redlich-Soave-Kwong all have this shape. One expression holds a gas and a liquid. A perfect gas cannot do a liquid. Tait cannot do a gas.

## One Impedance Fixes the Whole Reflection

How a wave splits at the interface comes down to acoustic impedance.

$$
Z = \rho c
$$

Going from medium 1 into medium 2, the pressure reflection and transmission coefficients are these.

$$
R = \frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}, \qquad T = 1 + R = \frac{2 Z_2}{Z_2 + Z_1}
$$

$R$ is the reflected pressure-amplitude ratio, $T$ the transmitted one. Not density alone, not sound speed alone. Only the product.

Air and water give $Z_{\text{air}} = 416$, $Z_{\text{water}} = 1.50 \times 10^{6}$, a ratio of 3600. $R = 0.9994$, so 99.89% of the energy comes back. $T = 1.999$, and the interface pressure nearly doubles. That product is why sliding $\rho_2$ and $c_2$ in opposite directions, in the figure above, changed nothing.

The wave the interface sends back meets the outlet of the domain once more. Whether the outlet swallows it or returns it is set by [the $\sigma$ in NSCBC](/en/posts/2026-08-04-nscbc-nonreflecting-outlet-sigma/).

## A Smeared Interface Invents a Sound Speed That Does Not Exist

Spread the interface over a few cells and you get the situation every diffuse-interface method lives with.

When one cell holds gas and liquid, density and compressibility average like this.

$$
\rho_m = \alpha \rho_g + (1-\alpha)\rho_\ell, \qquad
\frac{1}{\rho_m c_m^{2}} = \frac{\alpha}{\rho_g c_g^{2}} + \frac{1-\alpha}{\rho_\ell c_\ell^{2}}
$$

$\alpha$ is the gas volume fraction and the subscripts $g$/$\ell$ mean gas and liquid. Wood's relation. The mixture is as soft as the gas and as heavy as the liquid.

At $\alpha = 0.5$, $c_m = 24$ m/s. One-fourteenth of air, one-sixtieth of water. No material in the problem sends a signal at that speed. The average made the number. The race that widens the smear by hand sits just after the printed table.

## What Solving in Primitive Variables Costs

The debatable choice is to advance primitive variables $(p, \vec{u}, T)$ rather than the conserved set $(\rho, \rho\vec{u}, \rho E)$. The energy equation is rewritten as a pressure equation.

What is gained is agreement of the two pressures, and an implicit heat-conduction term. The pressure from the equation of state and the pressure from the semi-implicit correction match every step. In conserved variables those two are known to drift. Conduction can also be treated implicitly, which matters in a low-Mach problem where heat leads, such as natural convection.

What is lost is conservation of total energy. The solver therefore cuts its own range at subsonic flow. Capturing a shock needs a different formulation.

Pressure positivity is not guaranteed either. The authors read that as room, not as a defect. A metastable liquid in cavitation really does sit at negative pressure, and an equation of state like van der Waals can pair a negative pressure with a positive temperature.

## Reflection and Wood's Speed, Printed

The numbers behind the figures, printed directly.

```python
# Reflection/transmission at an air-water interface, and Wood's mixture sound speed
from math import sqrt

AIR   = (1.2,   347.0)    # (rho, c)
WATER = (998.0, 1500.0)

def impedance_split(m1, m2):
    z1, z2 = m1[0] * m1[1], m2[0] * m2[1]
    R = (z2 - z1) / (z2 + z1)
    return z1, z2, R, 1.0 + R, R * R

def wood_speed(alpha, gas=AIR, liq=WATER):
    rho_m = alpha * gas[0] + (1.0 - alpha) * liq[0]
    inv   = alpha / (gas[0] * gas[1] ** 2) + (1.0 - alpha) / (liq[0] * liq[1] ** 2)
    return 1.0 / sqrt(rho_m * inv)

z1, z2, R, T, E = impedance_split(AIR, WATER)
print(f"Z_air = {z1:8.1f}   Z_water = {z2:10.1f}   Z2/Z1 = {z2/z1:6.0f}")
print(f"R = {R:.5f}   T = 1+R = {T:.5f}   reflected energy = {100*E:.3f} %")
print(f"velocity amp in water / incident = {(z1/z2)*T:.3e}")

print("\nalpha   c_wood [m/s]")
for a in (0.0, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 1.0):
    print(f"{a:5.2f}   {wood_speed(a):8.1f}")
```

## A Droplet in a Standing Wave — the Paper's Last Test

The last test puts a 1 mm droplet in a cylindrical cavity of height $L = 8$ mm and drives the top boundary with a pressure oscillation.

$$
p(t) = p_0 + \Delta p \sin(\omega t)
$$

$\omega = 2\pi f$ and $f = c_0/(2L) \approx 21.7$ kHz is the cavity's first acoustic mode. $\Delta p$ is 2% of $p_0$, 2026 Pa. Gravity is zero.

Once the standing wave stands, a pressure node sits at $z = L/2$. A pressure node is a velocity antinode. The velocity amplitude follows from the impedance.

$$
\Delta v = \frac{\Delta p}{\rho_0 c_0} \approx \frac{2026}{416} \approx 4.9\ \text{m/s}
$$

The droplet sits in the middle of that oscillating velocity field. Pressure still propagates inside the liquid, and the initial circular drop holds a Laplace jump $2\sigma/r = 116$ Pa. The gap between the 2026 Pa acoustic disturbance and the 116 Pa the surface tension is defending decides the result. Each period the drop stretches and squeezes along the axis, and a large enough amplitude ends in breakup.

That picture is why the formulation above was needed. An incompressible liquid deletes the pressure wave inside the drop. A smeared interface lets a 24 m/s fake sound speed in the nearby cells shift the node. A sharp interface, with both phases compressible, is what produces the picture.

## The One-Cell Smear That Shoves the Node

The 24 m/s at $\alpha = 0.5$ is the number a solver steps on if the interface is left smeared. A five-equation model that keeps that sound speed on purpose is [the Wood speed in Murrone–Guillard](/en/posts/2026-10-18-murrone-guillard-wood-sound-speed/), which carries the same 24 m/s along.
