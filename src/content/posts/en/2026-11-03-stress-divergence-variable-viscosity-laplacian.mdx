## The pressure pushed with 1, the walls resisted 1.5

I drove a parallel channel with a single pressure gradient. The top wall was heated, so its viscosity was one tenth of the bottom wall's. At steady state, the shear on the two walls must add up to the pressure force. The computation said otherwise: the walls were holding back 1.5 times the pressure force. The flow rate came out 17.7% above the analytic solution.

The bug was one line. The viscous term was written as $\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$, straight from the textbook incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. This post rederives that term from a single fluid element. It shows exactly where the "constant viscosity" assumption sneaks in. At the end there is a one-line test that tells you which form your own code uses.

## Newton's second law on one fluid element

Cut out a small box of fluid. Replace mass by density, and Newton's second law becomes a per-unit-volume statement.

$$
\rho \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \rho\,\mathbf{g} + \mathbf{f}_{\text{surf}}
$$

$D/Dt$ is the material derivative (rate of change following the element), $\rho\mathbf{g}$ is the body force, and $\mathbf{f}_{\text{surf}}$ is the surface force per unit volume.

The surface force comes from stresses on the six faces. The stress $\sigma_{ij}$ is the force in direction $j$ on a face normal to axis $i$. For an element at rest or in uniform motion, front-face and back-face stresses cancel. An accelerating element means they don't. The net force in $x$ reduces to three derivatives.

$$
f_x\,dx\,dy\,dz = \left(\frac{\partial \sigma_{xx}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \sigma_{yx}}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial \sigma_{zx}}{\partial z}\right) dx\,dy\,dz
$$

The bracket is the divergence of the stress tensor. The divergence of a tensor is a vector. So the equation of motion collapses to one line.

$$
\rho \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \rho\,\mathbf{g} + \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}, \qquad \sigma_{ij} = -p\,\delta_{ij} + \tau_{ij}
$$

$p$ is pressure, $\tau_{ij}$ is the viscous stress, and $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. Nothing has been assumed about viscosity yet. All we have is "viscous force = difference of face stresses."

## Why the stress tensor must be symmetric

Suppose $\tau_{xy}$ and $\tau_{yx}$ differ. The difference produces a torque about $z$. Torque scales like area × lever arm, or $dx^3$. Moment of inertia scales like mass × length², or $dx^5$.

$$
\dot\omega_z \approx \frac{12\,(\tau_{xy}-\tau_{yx})}{\rho\,(dx^2+dy^2)}
$$

$\dot\omega_z$ is the angular acceleration of the element. Shrink the element to zero and the angular acceleration blows up. A continuum only makes sense if $\tau_{xy}=\tau_{yx}$. The same argument holds for all three axes. The stress tensor is symmetric, with six independent components.

## The two conditions that turn ∇·τ into μ∇²u

For a Newtonian fluid (stress linear in strain rate), the constitutive law is:

$$
\tau_{ij} = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) + \lambda\,\delta_{ij}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}
$$

$\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity and $\lambda$ the second viscosity. The bracket is twice the strain-rate tensor. A fluid at rest does not deform, so its viscous stress is zero. The $\mu$ here is the same proportionality constant discussed in [the kinetic origin of viscosity](/en/posts/2026-05-14-viscosity-kinetic-origin-transport-analogy/).

Now take the divergence, leaving $\mu$ inside the derivative.

$$
\frac{\partial \tau_{ij}}{\partial x_j} = \mu\,\nabla^2 u_i + \mu\,\frac{\partial (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})}{\partial x_i} + \frac{\partial \mu}{\partial x_j}\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right) + \frac{\partial (\lambda\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})}{\partial x_i}
$$

Keeping only the first term takes two conditions. One is incompressibility, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$, which kills the second and fourth terms. The other is $\nabla\mu=0$, constant viscosity. Without it, the third term survives.

In a parallel channel $u(y)$ it gets even simpler. With pressure gradient $G=-dp/dx$, the steady momentum equation reads:

$$
0 = G + \frac{d}{dy}\left(\mu\,\frac{du}{dy}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \tau(y) = \mu\,\frac{du}{dy} = C - G\,y
$$

$C$ is a constant fixed by the wall conditions $u(0)=u(1)=0$. Shear stress is a straight line no matter how $\mu$ varies. Viscosity only changes the velocity gradient. The $\mu\nabla^2u$ form instead solves $\mu u''=-G$. The difference between the two is exactly $\mu' u'$. With constant viscosity, the channel falls back to the [analytic Poiseuille solution](/en/posts/2026-03-28-poiseuille-flow-analytical-solution/).

## Code: a heated channel, two viscous terms

Take a dimensionless channel, $H=1$, $G=1$, with viscosity varying exponentially from 1 at the bottom wall to $1/r$ at the top. March in time from rest to steady state. Only the viscous term changes. `div` builds $\mu\,\partial u/\partial y$ on cell faces and differences it. `lap` multiplies a second difference by the cell-center $\mu$. At the end, the code prints the momentum budget of the interior control volume (pressure force minus shear on the two end faces) as a residual.

```python
import numpy as np

# dimensionless channel: H=1, rho=1, pressure gradient G=-dp/dx=1
# viscosity decays exponentially from 1 at the bottom wall to 1/r at the top (heated top wall)
N = 41
y = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, N)
dy = y[1] - y[0]

def trap(f, x):
    return float(np.sum(0.5 * (f[1:] + f[:-1]) * np.diff(x)))

def mu_profile(yy, r):
    return np.exp(-np.log(r) * yy)

def exact_heated_channel(r, m=20001):
    # mu u' = C - y,  u(0)=u(1)=0  → C = ∫y/mu / ∫1/mu
    s = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, m)
    w = 1.0 / mu_profile(s, r)
    C = trap(s * w, s) / trap(w, s)
    du = (C - s) * w
    u = np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(0.5 * (du[1:] + du[:-1]) * (s[1] - s[0]))])
    return trap(u, s), C, -(C - 1.0)   # Q, tau_bottom, tau_top(force the wall exerts on the fluid)

def viscous_term(u, mu_c, mu_f, form):
    if form == "div":       # ∂y(mu ∂y u): difference of face stresses
        tau_f = mu_f * (u[1:] - u[:-1]) / dy
        return (tau_f[1:] - tau_f[:-1]) / dy
    else:                   # mu ∇²u: cell-center viscosity x curvature
        return mu_c[1:-1] * (u[2:] - 2 * u[1:-1] + u[:-2]) / dy**2

def time_march_channel(r, form, tol=1e-10):
    mu_c = mu_profile(y, r)
    mu_f = mu_profile(0.5 * (y[1:] + y[:-1]), r)
    dt = 0.4 * dy**2 / mu_c.max()
    u = np.zeros(N)
    for n in range(1, 2_000_000):
        du = dt * (1.0 + viscous_term(u, mu_c, mu_f, form))
        u[1:-1] += du
        if np.abs(du).max() / dt < tol:
            break
    Q = trap(u, y)
    tau_f = mu_f * (u[1:] - u[:-1]) / dy
    # momentum budget of interior control volume [dy/2, 1-dy/2]: pressure force = shear on the two end faces -> residual
    balance = 1.0 * (1.0 - dy) - (tau_f[0] - tau_f[-1])
    return Q, balance, n

print(" r    Q_exact   Q_div     Q_lap     err_lap%  balance_div  balance_lap  steps")
for r in [1, 2, 5, 10, 30]:
    Qe, tb, tt = exact_heated_channel(r)
    Qd, bd, nd = time_march_channel(r, "div")
    Ql, bl, nl = time_march_channel(r, "lap")
    print(f"{r:3d}  {Qe:.5f}  {Qd:.5f}  {Ql:.5f}  {100*(Ql-Qe)/Qe:+7.1f}  {bd:+.1e}     {bl:+.1e}       {nd}")
```

## Why 17.7% and −0.5 share one cause

The `div` residual is $10^{-11}$, the convergence tolerance. That is no accident. Summing the viscous term over interior cells gives a telescoping series: each shared face stress cancels against its neighbor. Only the two end-face stresses remain. The "difference of face stresses" from the derivation survives in the discrete equations.

`lap` has no such structure. Each cell multiplies by its own center value $\mu_i$, so neighboring cells share no face stress. Nothing cancels in the sum. At $r=10$, that leaves a spurious body force inside the domain worth 50% of the pressure force. The walls hold back the pressure of 1 plus the fake 0.5. That is the −0.5 residual.

The flow-rate overshoot comes from the same term. The dropped piece is $\mu' u' = -\ln r\,(C-y)$. Its size scales with $\ln r$ and peaks near the walls. `lap` sets the curvature from local viscosity alone ($u''=-G/\mu$). Near the top wall, where viscosity is $1/r$, the curvature grows $r$-fold, and that accumulates into a bloated profile across the whole channel. At $r=30$ the error grows to 39% and the residual to −1.3, a fake force larger than the pressure itself.

The −0.1% at $r=1$ is a different kind of error. Both forms agree there, and it shrinks with grid refinement. It is just discretization error of the second difference.

## Where this error actually hides

Temperature-dependent viscosity (heated walls, lubricants, magma) is the most common case. RANS solvers are another. The eddy viscosity $\mu_t$ is zero at the wall and grows by hundreds outward. There, in $\nabla\cdot[(\mu+\mu_t)(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^T)]$, the transpose term $\nabla\mathbf{u}^T$ must not be dropped either. It vanishes in parallel flow but comes back in 2D separated flow. Multiphase interfaces are worse still, with viscosity ratios above 100.

Finite-volume codes (FVM) write face fluxes, so they are usually in `div` form. The risky places are finite-difference codes and codes that reuse a Laplacian operator "because the flow is incompressible." OpenFOAM's `fvm::laplacian(nuEff, U)` is also a `div` form using face viscosity. The transpose term is added separately as `fvc::div(nuEff*dev2(T(fvc::grad(U))))`.

## One test to run before you drop the constant-viscosity assumption

Impose an exponential viscosity profile with ratio 10 and run to steady state. Compare the sum of the interior viscous terms with the difference of the two end-face stresses. If they match within the convergence tolerance, the form is conservative. If they differ by 0.5, a $\mu\nabla^2u$ is hiding somewhere. This residual catches it before the flow rate does. A 17.7% flow-rate error blends in with other errors; the residual is either zero or it isn't.
