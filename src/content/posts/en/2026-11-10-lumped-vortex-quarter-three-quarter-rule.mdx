## One panel, and the lift came out exact

I modeled a flat plate at 5° angle of attack with a single point vortex. One panel, nothing more. The lift coefficient matched the theoretical $2\pi\alpha$ to four decimal places. Then I moved the control point half a slot forward. Now even 64 panels gave exactly half the lift.

The intuition that refinement shrinks error simply fails here. This post derives where the textbook rule "vortex at 1/4, control point at 3/4" comes from. Then it measures, in code, what breaks when you move them. By the end you can audit the placement in any vortex-lattice (VLM) or panel code with a single number.

## What thin-airfoil theory asks for: a vortex sheet γ(x)

Thin-airfoil theory replaces the airfoil with a vortex sheet (a continuous distribution of vorticity) along the chord line. Call its strength $\gamma(x)$. There is one condition: no flow may pass through the airfoil surface.

$$
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^c \frac{\gamma(x')\,dx'}{x-x'} = V_\infty\left(\alpha - \frac{dz}{dx}\right), \qquad 0<x<c
$$

$V_\infty$ is the freestream speed, $\alpha$ the angle of attack, $z(x)$ the camber line, $c$ the chord. The left side is the downwash (the downward normal velocity) induced by the sheet. Add the Kutta condition $\gamma(c)=0$ at the trailing edge.

The flat-plate ($z=0$) solution is classic.

$$
\gamma(x) = 2V_\infty\alpha\sqrt{\frac{c-x}{x}}, \qquad \Gamma = \int_0^c\gamma\,dx = \pi c V_\infty\alpha
$$

$\Gamma$ is the total circulation. Plug it into the Kutta–Joukowski theorem $L'=\rho V_\infty\Gamma$ and you get $C_L = 2\pi\alpha$. The link between circulation and lift is covered in [circulation and the Kutta–Joukowski theorem](/en/posts/2026-05-07-circulation-lift-kutta-joukowski/). One more fact matters. The centroid of this distribution sits exactly at $c/4$. That is why the flat plate has zero moment about the quarter chord.

## One vortex, one control point — where the half-chord rule comes from

Replace the continuous sheet with one point vortex at $x=a$. Put the control point (collocation point, where the no-penetration condition is enforced) at $x=b$. A point vortex induces speed $\Gamma/(2\pi r)$ at distance $r$. At the control point the downwash must equal $V_\infty\alpha$.

$$
\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi(b-a)} = V_\infty\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Gamma = 2\pi(b-a)V_\infty\alpha
$$

For this $\Gamma$ to equal the exact $\pi cV_\infty\alpha$, you need $b-a=c/2$. Lift depends on the gap alone. The vortex position $a$ never enters the lift.

$a$ shows up in the moment. The leading-edge moment is $M_{LE}=-\rho V_\infty\Gamma a$. The exact sheet's resultant acts at $c/4$, so the moment is right only when $a=c/4$. Combine both conditions: $a=c/4$, $b=3c/4$. That is the textbook lumped-vortex rule. In terms of the pressure jump $\Delta C_p = 2\gamma/V_\infty$ it says the same thing. One point vortex is placed to match two numbers: the size of the resultant and where it acts.

So far this is a one-panel story. What happens with $N$ panels, each holding a vortex and control point at the same fractions? Intuition says the placement should stop mattering as $N$ grows.

## Code: sweeping vortex and control-point positions over 1–64 panels

Split the chord into $N$ equal panels. Panel $j$ carries its vortex at $x_j + a\,\Delta x$ and its control point at $x_j + b\,\Delta x$. Enforcing no-penetration at every control point gives an $N\times N$ linear system. Experiment one fixes $a=0.25$ on a flat plate and varies only $b$. Experiment two compares three placements on a parabolic camber line $z=4hx(1-x)$.

```python
import numpy as np

def lumped_vortex(N, a, b, alpha, h=0.0):
    """Thin airfoil (c=1, V=1): vortex at fraction a, control point at fraction b of each panel"""
    xe = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, N + 1)
    dx = xe[1:] - xe[:-1]
    xv = xe[:-1] + a * dx                  # vortex positions
    xc = xe[:-1] + b * dx                  # control-point positions
    slope = 4 * h * (1 - 2 * xc)           # parabolic camber z = 4h x(1-x)
    A = 1.0 / (2 * np.pi * (xc[:, None] - xv[None, :]))
    G = np.linalg.solve(A, alpha - slope)  # zero normal velocity at control points
    CL = 2 * G.sum()                       # Kutta-Joukowski
    Cm_le = -2 * (G * xv).sum()
    return CL, Cm_le

Ns = (1, 2, 4, 16, 64)
alpha = np.radians(5.0)
CL0 = 2 * np.pi * alpha
print(f"[1] flat plate, alpha=5deg, CL / 2*pi*alpha   (a = 0.25)")
print(f"{'b':>6} " + "".join(f"{'N=' + str(n):>9}" for n in Ns))
for b in (0.50, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85, 1.00):
    print(f"{b:6.2f} " + "".join(f"{lumped_vortex(n, 0.25, b, alpha)[0] / CL0:9.4f}" for n in Ns))

al, h = np.radians(2.0), 0.04
CLx = 2 * np.pi * (al + 2 * h)
Cmx = -np.pi / 2 * (al + 4 * h)
print(f"\n[2] parabolic camber h=0.04c, alpha=2deg  exact CL={CLx:.4f}  Cm_le={Cmx:.4f}")
print(f"{'(a,b)':>11} " + "".join(f"{'N=' + str(n):>9}" for n in Ns) + "   <- CL error %")
for a, b in ((0.25, 0.75), (0.00, 0.50), (0.50, 1.00)):
    errs = [100 * (lumped_vortex(n, a, b, al, h)[0] / CLx - 1) for n in Ns]
    print(f"({a:.2f},{b:.2f}) " + "".join(f"{e:+9.2f}" for e in errs))
print(f"{'(a,b)':>11} " + "".join(f"{'N=' + str(n):>9}" for n in Ns) + "   <- Cm_le error %")
for a, b in ((0.25, 0.75), (0.00, 0.50), (0.50, 1.00)):
    errs = [100 * (lumped_vortex(n, a, b, al, h)[1] / Cmx - 1) for n in Ns]
    print(f"({a:.2f},{b:.2f}) " + "".join(f"{e:+9.2f}" for e in errs))
```

## Why 2(b−a) ignores the panel count

The numbers in table [1] are clean. Every row equals $2(b-a)$: 0.5 for $b=0.5$, 1.5 for $b=1.0$. Going to 64 panels changes nothing in the fourth decimal.

The reason is the self-similarity of a uniform grid. The influence coefficients look like $1/\big(2\pi\Delta x\,(i-j+b-a)\big)$. The whole matrix depends on the gap ratio $b-a$ alone. The factor-of-$2(b-a)$ error born on one panel is repeated, with the same structure, on every subdivision. However fine the grid, every panel applies the same wrong rule. It is a consistency error, and it does not converge away.

Conversely, with $b-a=1/2$ the total circulation is exact for any $N$. J. C. James showed this "remarkable accuracy" analytically in 1972. The individual discrete $\Gamma_j$ differ from the exact $\gamma$ integrated over each panel. Only their sum comes out exact.

## Add camber and only a = 1/4 survives

On the flat plate, $b-a=1/2$ was enough. Indeed $(0, 0.5)$ and $(0.5, 1.0)$ also give exact flat-plate lift in the code. Camber changes that. The exact parabolic-camber result is $C_L = 2\pi(\alpha + 2h/c)$.

$(0.25, 0.75)$ has 0.00% error starting at one panel. $(0, 0.5)$ starts at −69.62% and halves its error every time $N$ doubles. That is first-order convergence. $(0.5, 1.0)$ mirrors it with the opposite sign. Shift the vortex forward and lift comes out low; shift it aft and lift comes out high.

The camber slope $dz/dx$ varies along the chord. Moving the control point forward or aft samples a different slope. Vortex and control point must both sit in the right place for that sampling error to cancel. The balance point is $a=1/4$.

The moment behaves differently. For $(0.25, 0.75)$ the $C_{m,LE}$ error goes −41.05 → −10.26 → −2.57, shrinking by four each time. That is second order. With one panel the moment is off by 41%. A flat plate's resultant acts at $c/4$, but camber adds a pure moment $C_{m,c/4} = -\pi h/c$ about the quarter chord. A single point vortex cannot represent that couple. The other two placements converge only at first order in moment as well.

## The 1/4–3/4 rule inside vortex-lattice and panel methods

This rule doesn't end in textbook exercises. A 3D vortex-lattice method puts the bound leg of each horseshoe vortex at the panel's quarter chord. The control point sits at three-quarters. The result above is why even one chordwise panel gets the 2D section lift right.

Compare this with building the flow around a cylinder from a complex potential ([complex potential and d'Alembert's paradox](/en/posts/2026-05-21-complex-potential-cylinder-dalembert/)). There, conformal mapping gives an exact solution. Panel methods give up exactness and place discrete singularities instead. The placement rule then decides the accuracy.

Non-uniform grids need care. When panel lengths vary, as with cosine spacing, the self-similarity breaks. The 1/4–3/4 rule is then no longer "exact at any panel count." It becomes an ordinary approximation that converges with refinement.

## One ratio to print before you move a vortex

Auditing your own VLM or panel code is simple. Solve a flat plate with one panel and print $C_L/2\pi\alpha$. If it isn't 1, half of that value is the vortex-to-control-point gap you are actually using. A reading of 0.5000 means the control point sits a quarter panel from the vortex, not half. Refining the grid 64× will not remove that error.

Next, add parabolic camber and check that $C_L$ at $N=1$ matches $2\pi(\alpha+2h)$. If it does, the vortex is at the quarter point. This two-line test catches placement bugs before any grid-convergence study would.
