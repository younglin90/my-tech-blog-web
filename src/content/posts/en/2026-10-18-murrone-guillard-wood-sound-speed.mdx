## A one-line formula from a 1930 textbook

In 1930, A. B. Wood of the British Admiralty Research Laboratory published a textbook on sound. Tucked inside is a one-line formula for the sound speed of bubbly water. It says the compressibility of a mixture is the volume-weighted sum of the phase compressibilities. Plug in a 50/50 air–water foam and you get about 24 m/s. That is far slower than water (1625 m/s) and far slower than air (342 m/s).

This post reads the Murrone–Guillard INRIA report RR-4778 (2003). The report studies a reduced five-equation model (a model obtained from the seven-equation model in the limit of instantaneous relaxation) for compressible two-phase flow. One question drives the post: what does the $K\nabla\cdot u$ term in the volume-fraction equation actually do? We answer it with a 1D Python run. Same tube, same pulse, with and without $K$, and we measure the speed of sound.

The parent model, where each phase keeps its own velocity and pressure, is covered in the [Baer–Nunziato seven-equation post](/en/posts/2026-06-27-baer-nunziato-seven-equation-two-phase/). Today we look at what survives when that model is reduced to the limit.

## The question the 2003 report reopened

Wood's formula is old news. The real question is whether a numerical model reproduces it on its own. The seven-equation Baer–Nunziato model carries separate velocities and pressures for both phases. It is physically rich but expensive. If the phases lock to a common velocity and pressure quickly, you can drop variables.

Kapila et al. (2001) derived a five-equation model in this limit. Murrone and Guillard rederived it with an asymptotic expansion (expanding the solution in powers of a small parameter). They then worked out its hyperbolicity (real, finite wave speeds) and wave structure, and tested two numerical schemes.

One figure in the report is the starting point here. It plots the air–water mixture sound speed against the air volume fraction. There are two curves. "5 Eq. Reduc" is the reduced model. "5 Eq. Trans" is a model that only transports the volume fraction. The two curves are far apart.

## Five equations left after infinitely fast relaxation

Send the velocity and pressure relaxation times of the seven-equation model to zero and this remains:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\partial_t(\alpha_1\rho_1) + \nabla\cdot(\alpha_1\rho_1 u) = 0, \qquad \partial_t(\alpha_2\rho_2) + \nabla\cdot(\alpha_2\rho_2 u) = 0,\\
&\partial_t(\rho u) + \nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u + p I) = 0, \qquad \partial_t(\rho E) + \nabla\cdot\big((\rho E + p)u\big) = 0,\\
&\partial_t\alpha_1 + u\cdot\nabla\alpha_1 = K\,\nabla\cdot u .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\alpha_k$ is the volume fraction of phase $k$, $\rho_k$ its density, $u$ and $p$ the shared velocity and pressure, $E$ the mixture total energy. The first four equations are conservative. Only the last one is not, and its right-hand side $K\nabla\cdot u$ is the subject of this post.

## Where K∇·u comes from

In pressure equilibrium both phases see the same pressure change. Away from shocks, phase $k$ is compressed isentropically, so $D\ln\rho_k/Dt = (\rho_k c_k^2)^{-1} Dp/Dt$. Combine this with phase mass conservation, $D(\alpha_k\rho_k)/Dt = -\alpha_k\rho_k\nabla\cdot u$:

$$
\frac{D\alpha_k}{Dt} = -\alpha_k\nabla\cdot u - \frac{\alpha_k}{\rho_k c_k^2}\frac{Dp}{Dt}
$$

Add the two phase equations. Since $\alpha_1+\alpha_2=1$, the left side vanishes and a pressure equation drops out:

$$
\frac{Dp}{Dt} = -\rho c_W^2\,\nabla\cdot u, \qquad \frac{1}{\rho c_W^2} = \frac{\alpha_1}{\rho_1 c_1^2} + \frac{\alpha_2}{\rho_2 c_2^2}
$$

$c_W$ is Wood's sound speed. Substitute it back and $K$ is fixed:

$$
K = \frac{\alpha_1\alpha_2\,(\rho_2 c_2^2 - \rho_1 c_1^2)}{\alpha_1\rho_2 c_2^2 + \alpha_2\rho_1 c_1^2}
$$

$\rho_k c_k^2$ is the bulk modulus (resistance to compression) of phase $k$. Water is about 19,000 times stiffer than air. With air as phase 1, $K\approx\alpha_1$. When the mixture is squeezed, the air absorbs almost all of the volume change.

## Wood's speed: compressibility by volume, inertia by density

Wood's speed is low because two properties come from different phases. The soft air sets the compressibility. The heavy water sets the inertia. At 50% air, the compressibility is about half that of pure air. The density is about 400 times that of air. Since sound speed scales as $\sqrt{1/(\text{density}\times\text{compressibility})}$, it drops to roughly $1/\sqrt{200}$ of the air sound speed.

Set $K=0$ and the story changes. The volume fraction just rides with the flow. The mixture then behaves like a single stiffened gas (an equation of state that approximates stiff liquids like water). Its sound speed follows $\rho\xi c^2 = \sum_k \alpha_k\xi_k\rho_k c_k^2$ with $\xi_k = 1/(\gamma_k-1)$. Water's huge bulk modulus leaks in through an energy weighting. So sound stays fast even at 50% air.

## Code: two models in the same tube

Equations of state: air $\gamma=1.4,\ \pi=0$, water $\gamma=4.4,\ \pi=6\times10^8$ Pa. Fill a 1 m tube with a 50% air mixture. Place a Gaussian pressure pulse with 0.1% amplitude at the center. Run until the right-going pulse should have moved 0.3 m, then measure the speed from the peak position. Conservative variables use a Rusanov flux. The volume fraction uses central differences plus artificial diffusion of the same size.

```python
import math

# stiffened gas: phase 1 = air, phase 2 = water
G = (1.4, 4.4)
PI = (0.0, 6.0e8)
P0 = 1.0e5
RHO0 = (1.2, 1000.0)

def wood_speed(a1):
    rho = a1 * RHO0[0] + (1 - a1) * RHO0[1]
    comp = a1 / (G[0] * (P0 + PI[0])) + (1 - a1) / (G[1] * (P0 + PI[1]))
    return 1.0 / math.sqrt(rho * comp)

def isobaric_speed(a1):
    """alpha only advected (K = 0): the mixture is one stiffened gas"""
    rho = a1 * RHO0[0] + (1 - a1) * RHO0[1]
    xi = a1 / (G[0] - 1) + (1 - a1) / (G[1] - 1)
    gpi = a1 * G[0] * PI[0] / (G[0] - 1) + (1 - a1) * G[1] * PI[1] / (G[1] - 1)
    gam = 1 + 1 / xi
    return math.sqrt(gam * (P0 + gpi / xi / gam) / rho)

def mix_pressure(a1, rhoe):
    a2 = 1 - a1
    num = rhoe - a1 * G[0] * PI[0] / (G[0] - 1) - a2 * G[1] * PI[1] / (G[1] - 1)
    return num / (a1 / (G[0] - 1) + a2 / (G[1] - 1))

def kapila_K(a1, p):
    k1, k2 = G[0] * (p + PI[0]), G[1] * (p + PI[1])
    a2 = 1 - a1
    return a1 * a2 * (k2 - k1) / (a1 * k2 + a2 * k1)

def pulse_speed(a_bg, use_K, n=400, travel=0.3, amp=1e-3):
    dx = 1.0 / n
    x = [(i + 0.5) * dx for i in range(n)]
    a1 = [a_bg] * n
    p = [P0 * (1 + amp * math.exp(-((xi - 0.5) / 0.03) ** 2)) for xi in x]
    m1 = [a_bg * RHO0[0]] * n
    m2 = [(1 - a_bg) * RHO0[1]] * n
    mom = [0.0] * n
    E = [a_bg * (pi + G[0] * PI[0]) / (G[0] - 1) + (1 - a_bg) * (pi + G[1] * PI[1]) / (G[1] - 1) for pi in p]
    c = wood_speed(a_bg) if use_K else isobaric_speed(a_bg)
    s = 1.05 * c                        # Rusanov speed = the model's own sound speed
    t_end = travel / c
    steps = math.ceil(t_end / (0.4 * dx / s))
    dt = t_end / steps
    g = lambda q, i: q[min(max(i, 0), n - 1)]   # zero-gradient ghost cells
    for _ in range(steps):
        u = [mom[i] / (m1[i] + m2[i]) for i in range(n)]
        pr = [mix_pressure(a1[i], E[i] - 0.5 * mom[i] * u[i]) for i in range(n)]
        U = (m1, m2, mom, E)
        F = ([m1[i] * u[i] for i in range(n)], [m2[i] * u[i] for i in range(n)],
             [mom[i] * u[i] + pr[i] for i in range(n)], [(E[i] + pr[i]) * u[i] for i in range(n)])
        new = []
        for q, f in zip(U, F):
            fl = [0.5 * (g(f, i - 1) + g(f, i)) - 0.5 * s * (g(q, i) - g(q, i - 1)) for i in range(n + 1)]
            new.append([q[i] - dt / dx * (fl[i + 1] - fl[i]) for i in range(n)])
        na = []
        for i in range(n):
            K = kapila_K(a1[i], pr[i]) if use_K else 0.0
            dudx = (g(u, i + 1) - g(u, i - 1)) / (2 * dx)
            dadx = (g(a1, i + 1) - g(a1, i - 1)) / (2 * dx)
            lap = g(a1, i + 1) - 2 * a1[i] + g(a1, i - 1)
            na.append(a1[i] + dt * (-u[i] * dadx + K * dudx + s * lap / (2 * dx)))
        m1, m2, mom, E = new
        a1 = na
    pr = [mix_pressure(a1[i], E[i] - 0.5 * mom[i] ** 2 / (m1[i] + m2[i])) for i in range(n)]
    ipk = max((i for i in range(n) if x[i] > 0.6), key=lambda i: pr[i])
    return c, (x[ipk] - 0.5) / t_end, steps

print("alpha_air  wood[m/s]  K=0[m/s]")
for a in [0.0, 1e-4, 1e-3, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 1.0]:
    print(f"{a:9.4f}  {wood_speed(a):9.1f}  {isobaric_speed(a):8.1f}")
amin = min((k / 1000 for k in range(1, 1000)), key=wood_speed)
print(f"Wood minimum: {wood_speed(amin):.2f} m/s at alpha_air = {amin:.3f}")
print(f"K at alpha_air=0.5: {kapila_K(0.5, P0):.6f}")
print("\n1D pulse, alpha_air = 0.5, N = 400")
for useK in (True, False):
    c, meas, st = pulse_speed(0.5, useK)
    tag = "with K" if useK else "K = 0 "
    print(f"{tag}  theory {c:7.2f}  measured {meas:7.2f}  ratio {meas / c:.3f}  steps {st}")
```

## What 23.55 and 742.19 tell us

Both runs share the grid, the flux, and the initial pulse. Only the $K$ line differs. Yet the pulse speeds split by a factor of 31.5: 23.55 m/s versus 742.19 m/s. Each matches its model's theoretical sound speed within 0.4%. That 0.4% is first-order Rusanov diffusion dragging the peak back.

So the scheme is not wrong. The two models solve different physics. The $K=0$ model transports this pulse accurately, just at the wrong speed. No amount of grid refinement brings 742 down to 23. We saw how deleting $K$ over-compresses water near an interface in the [compressible VOF K-term post](/en/posts/2026-10-03-compressible-vof-compressibility-factor/). Today's numbers are the footprint of the same term inside a uniform mixture.

## From 1% to 99% air: where the curves part

The table shows three things. First, just 0.01% air drops Wood's speed from 1625 to 957 m/s. At 1% air it is 119 m/s. A few bubbles rewrite the acoustics of water.

Second, the minimum of 23.65 m/s sits at $\alpha\approx0.5$. The larger the bulk-modulus contrast, the closer that minimum hugs 0.5.

Third, the $K=0$ curve reads 540.9 m/s at 99% air. That is faster than pure air at 341.6 m/s. It claims 1% water makes the mixture stiffer than air itself, which is unphysical. The energy-weighted equation of state lets water's $\pi$ leak into the air. This is also where the report's "5 Eq. Trans" curve departs from the reduced model.

Splitting the sound speed into subsystems to move stiffness around is a separate approach, reviewed in the [Toro–Vázquez splitting post](/en/posts/2026-10-10-toro-vazquez-split-pressure-wave-speed/). Today's two curves come before any such splitting: they are about which sound speed the model itself has.

## What the report checks against experiment

The report does not stop at sound-speed curves. It plots phase densities and internal energies from an air–water shock tube for both models side by side. It then plots the shock Hugoniot of an epoxy–spinel powder mixture, the relation between shock speed $U_s$ and particle speed $U_p$. The figure overlays the reduced five-equation model, the seven-equation model, and experimental points.

That comparison matters for a reason. $K\nabla\cdot u$ is non-conservative, so the model alone does not uniquely fix the shock jump conditions. The form of numerical viscosity can shift the post-shock state. Hence the need to check, figure by figure, how close the reduced model stays to the seven-equation model and to experiment. For weak waves, as in today's run, the issue disappears. For strong shocks it needs its own validation.

## How to tell whether your five-equation code uses K

The cheapest test is today's pulse test. Put a small pressure pulse in a uniform $\alpha=0.5$ mixture and time it. Around 23 m/s means $K$ is alive. Several hundred m/s means the volume fraction is only being transported.

Check the sound speed in your CFL condition too. Feed a frozen sound speed (a simple average-like combination of phase speeds) into a code that propagates at Wood's speed and your time step becomes tens of times too conservative. Feed Wood's speed into a $K=0$ model and you violate CFL.

Finally, look at regions with very little gas. At 0.1% air, Wood's speed is 365 m/s, already below a quarter of the water value. In early-stage cavitation or pumps ingesting bubbles, this sound speed sets pressure-wave arrival times. If your code reports 1600 m/s there, it is time to revisit the model.
