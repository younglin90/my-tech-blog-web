In 1742 Benjamin Robins watched musket balls refuse a straight line. Same gun, same powder, same aim. Some rounds went left. Some went right. The shot was spinning as it left the muzzle. Spin and deflection arrived as a pair.

A century later Gustav Magnus fixed the side force in an experiment. A rotating body drifts sideways in a flow. The quantity that writes the size of that force is circulation.

## An old headache called the curving cannonball

Gunners knew it before Robins. Cannonballs miss. Not only because of wind. Casting tolerances left a gap between the bore and the ball (the period term was *windage*), and at the moment of firing the ball ricocheted off the barrel wall and picked up an unpredictable spin. That spin bent its flight.

The stakes were high. Ballistics was a calculation that decided the survival of nations. Newton derived a parabolic trajectory in the *Principia*, but real shot left the parabola. Robins separated that departure experimentally: part of it was air drag, part of it was spin.

The core question is this. Why does spin create a sideways force?

## The sideways push comes from circulation

Start with the magnitude. When a rotating cylinder sits in a uniform stream of speed $U_\infty$, the sideways force per unit length is astonishingly simple.

$$
L' = \rho\, U_\infty\, \Gamma
$$

Here $\rho$ is the fluid density, $U_\infty$ the freestream speed, and $\Gamma$ the circulation. This relation is the [Kutta–Joukowski theorem that ties lift to circulation](/en/posts/2026-05-07-circulation-lift-kutta-joukowski/). The lift depends only on the circulation. The body's shape enters only through how much circulation it generates.

Circulation is the velocity integrated around a closed loop.

$$
\Gamma = \oint_C \mathbf{u}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell}
$$

$\mathbf{u}$ is the flow velocity and $d\boldsymbol{\ell}$ is the tangent element along the path. A nonzero circulation means there is a net swirling flow around the body. A rotating cylinder of radius $a$ and angular speed $\omega$ drags the fluid at its surface and builds exactly this net swirl.

$$
\Gamma = 2\pi a^2 \omega
$$

The direction is fixed by a cross product.

$$
\mathbf{F}_M \propto \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{U}_\infty
$$

$\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the angular velocity vector. This expression is the key that later unlocks the secret of rifling.

Because $\Gamma=2\pi a^2\omega$, doubling $\omega$ doubles the circulation. The steady side force $L'=\rho U_\infty\Gamma$ doubles with it. Shape does not appear in that line. The radius $a$ enters as a square, so the same spin rate builds circulation faster on a larger ball. The direction is $\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{U}_\infty$. Parallel vectors give a zero product and the side force drops out. The alignment rifling uses later is that one line.

## Why spin actually creates circulation

Where does the circulation come from? The answer lies in viscosity and the no-slip condition (the fluid at a wall moves with the wall).

One face of a spinning ball turns in the same direction as the flow; the opposite face turns against it. On the co-rotating side the surface pushes the boundary layer along, so the flow separates later (delayed separation). On the counter-rotating side the flow separates early. The net result is a wake that tilts to one side.

If the wake tilts downward, the fluid gains downward momentum. By reaction, the ball is pushed upward. That is the essence of the Magnus force. The ball deflects the flow one way and pays for it with a force the other way.

## The spin ratio decides how much it curves

Set the Reynolds number aside for a moment. The dimensionless number that governs the strength of the Magnus effect is the spin ratio.

$$
S = \frac{a\,\omega}{U_\infty}
$$

The numerator is the surface speed, the denominator the freestream speed. $S$ tells you "how many times faster the ball's surface turns than the wind blows." As $S$ grows, so does the circulation and the lift coefficient. A baseball curveball runs around $S \approx 0.2\text{–}0.4$; a hard bent soccer kick is higher.

Let's integrate a simple two-dimensional flight and plot the trajectory. Only drag and the Magnus force go in.

```python
import numpy as np

def magnus_trajectory(u0, spin, cm=0.16, cd=0.006, dt=0.012, steps=900):
    # Top-down plane. x is forward, y is sideways.
    pos = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
    vel = np.array([u0, 0.0])   # launched straight
    path = [pos.copy()]
    for _ in range(steps):
        sp = np.hypot(*vel) + 1e-9
        # Magnus: perpendicular to velocity (-vy, vx), sign set by spin
        a_magnus = cm * spin * np.array([-vel[1], vel[0]])
        a_drag   = -cd * sp * vel          # drag: opposite the velocity
        vel = vel + (a_magnus + a_drag) * dt
        pos = pos + vel * dt
        path.append(pos.copy())
        if pos[0] > 30:
            break
    return np.array(path)

for s in (0.0, 1.0, 2.0):
    p = magnus_trajectory(14.0, s)
    print(f"spin={s}:  sideways miss = {p[-1, 1]:6.2f} m")
# spin=0.0:  sideways miss =   0.00 m
# spin=1.0:  sideways miss =   3.71 m
# spin=2.0:  sideways miss =   6.90 m
```

Doubling the spin does not exactly double the miss. When the path curves, the velocity direction changes, so the force direction keeps rotating too. It is nonlinear.

The run uses $u_0=14$, $c_m=0.16$, $c_d=0.006$, $\Delta t=0.012$, at most 900 steps, and stops once $x>30$. The printout is a sideways miss of 0.00 m at spin 0, 3.71 m at spin 1, and 6.90 m at spin 2. Twice 3.71 is 7.42. The printed 6.90 m is 0.52 m short of that. As soon as the path bends, the Magnus acceleration $(-v_y, v_x)$ turns with the velocity. The force is not locked to the original sideways axis, so the miss does not grow linearly. In the spin ratio $S=a\omega/U_\infty$, a smaller denominator raises $S$. Drop the launch speed and the same $\omega$ bends more. A baseball curveball sits near $S\approx 0.2\text{–}0.4$. Reynolds was set aside because the lift line has no Re in it.

## Rifling, the backward solution

Here comes the paradox. The fix Robins found was not to kill the spin but to control it. Cut spiral grooves into the barrel (rifling) so the round spins about its flight axis.

Why does this work? The Magnus force scales as $\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{U}_\infty$. The spin axis that rifling imposes is the flight direction, so $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{U}_\infty$. When two vectors are parallel, their cross product is zero. The sideways force disappears.

At the same time this axial spin gives gyroscopic stability, like a top. It binds the random spin that the round used to pick up as it tumbled into one fixed axis. The random Magnus vanishes and the round flies straight. Not by removing spin, but by aligning it to the angle where the sideways force is zero.

## Miss the flight axis with the rifling spin and the side force stays

The Magnus force is $\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{U}_\infty$. Rifling that puts the spin axis along the flight direction makes the two vectors parallel, and the cross product is zero. The side force drops out. That axial spin is also gyroscopic stability, like a top. It binds onto one axis the random spin the ball used to pick up off the barrel wall. The spin was not removed. It was turned to the angle where the side force is zero.

Nonzero circulation means a net swirl around the body. A cylinder builds that swirl at the surface as $\Gamma=2\pi a^2\omega$. A wake that tilts down gives the fluid downward momentum, and the ball is pushed up. Putting Reynolds in the line that writes the size of this force fights $S$. The [account of how the Reynolds number splits the scale of a flow](/en/posts/2026-04-21-reynolds-number-scale-fluid-mechanics/) belongs in separation, not in the line $L'=\rho U_\infty\Gamma$.
