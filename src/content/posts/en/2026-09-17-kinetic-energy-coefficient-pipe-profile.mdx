## Take only the mean velocity to Bernoulli and half the energy disappears

The same pipe carries the same flow rate. Divide the flow rate by the area and you get a mean
velocity $V$. Build $V^2/2g$ from it and drop that into the energy equation. Everyone does this.

In laminar pipe flow that number is exactly half of the real kinetic energy. The flow rate is
right, the area is right, and the energy is off by 100%. Today I track down where the gap comes
from, and why it shrinks to 3% in turbulent flow, by doing the integrals directly.

## What actually got integrated when the velocity head was defined

The kinetic energy flux through a section is the local kinetic energy multiplied by the local mass
flux, integrated over the area.

$$
\dot{E}_k = \int_A \frac{1}{2} u^2 \cdot \rho u \, dA = \frac{\rho}{2} \int_A u^3 \, dA
$$

Here $u$ is the local axial velocity, $\rho$ the density and $A$ the cross-sectional area. The
integrand carries $u$ **three** times: twice from the energy and once from carrying the mass.

The textbook energy equation, by contrast, writes $\dot{m} V^2/2$. Since $\dot{m} = \rho V A$, that
is $\rho V^3 A / 2$. The ratio of the two is the kinetic energy coefficient $\alpha$. The momentum
equation has the same problem, and there $u$ appears twice, giving $\beta$.

$$
\alpha = \frac{\int_A u^3 \, dA}{V^3 A}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\int_A u^2 \, dA}{V^2 A}
$$

Both coefficients approach 1 as the profile flattens. Flip the profile switch from laminar to defect law and the gap between the two ledger bars collapses from 2 toward about 1.03.

## Why the coefficient comes out at exactly 2 in laminar pipe flow

The Hagen–Poiseuille solution is a parabola: $u = U_c \left[1 - (r/R)^2\right]$ with $V = U_c/2$.
Substituting $s = r/R$ makes both the numerator and the denominator close in closed form.

$$
\int_A u^3 \, dA = 2\pi R^2 U_c^3 \int_0^1 (1-s^2)^3 s \, ds = \frac{\pi R^2 U_c^3}{4}
$$

Since $V^3 A = (U_c/2)^3 \pi R^2 = \pi R^2 U_c^3 / 8$, we get $\alpha = 2$. The same route gives
$\beta = 4/3$. Neither is an approximation or a fit — they are ratios of integers.

Open the numerator and the 2 stops looking like a fit. $(1-s^2)^3 s = s - 3s^3 + 3s^5 - s^7$, and the integral from 0 to 1 is $1/2 - 3/4 + 1/2 - 1/8 = 1/8$. Times $2\pi R^2 U_c^3$ that is $\pi R^2 U_c^3/4$. The denominator is $\pi R^2 U_c^3/8$. A quarter over an eighth is 2. $\beta$ is one power lower and stops at $4/3$, which the table prints as 1.3333. For $n=7$, $2n^2/((n+1)(2n+1)) = 98/120 = 0.81666\ldots$, the printed 0.8167.

An $\alpha$ of 2 does not mean "half the energy went missing". It means a **single number** called
the mean velocity cannot stand in for a cube integral. To follow that same parabola from the closed
form of the velocity, start with
[the Poiseuille analytical solution](/en/posts/2026-03-28-poiseuille-flow-analytical-solution/).
There the integral is the velocity. Here it is the cube of that velocity.

## Writing a turbulent profile with a single exponent

Turbulent pipe flow changes steeply only near the wall and is nearly flat through the core. The
power law approximates that shape with one exponent.

$$
\frac{\bar{u}}{U_c} = \left(\frac{y}{R}\right)^{1/n}, \qquad y = R - r
$$

Here $y$ is the distance from the wall and $n$ is an exponent that grows with the Reynolds number.
The usual choice is $n=7$. The mean velocity of this profile integrates in closed form.

$$
\frac{V}{U_c} = \frac{2n^2}{(n+1)(2n+1)}
$$

For $n=7$ that is $0.8167$. Set it against the laminar $0.5$ and you can see immediately how much
blunter the profile has become. Since $\alpha$ is the same kind of integral, knowing $n$ fixes it.

## Four profiles, one ledger, written out in Python

The three families — laminar, power law and defect law — go through the same trapezoid integrator.
No external packages, standard library only. With 40,000 radial divisions the laminar result
matches the analytic value to four decimal places.

```python
import math

R, M = 1.0, 40000                      # radius, number of radial divisions
dr = R / M
rs = [i * dr for i in range(M + 1)]
A = math.pi * R * R

def ring_integral(vals):
    """integrate f(r) over the section: ∫ f · 2πr dr (trapezoid)"""
    s = 0.0
    for i in range(M):
        a = vals[i] * 2.0 * math.pi * rs[i]
        b = vals[i + 1] * 2.0 * math.pi * rs[i + 1]
        s += 0.5 * (a + b) * dr
    return s

def ledger(u):
    """mean velocity, kinetic energy coefficient alpha, momentum coefficient beta"""
    vbar = ring_integral(u) / A
    al = ring_integral([x ** 3 for x in u]) / (vbar ** 3 * A)
    be = ring_integral([x ** 2 for x in u]) / (vbar ** 2 * A)
    return vbar, al, be

def poiseuille():
    return [1.0 - (r / R) ** 2 for r in rs]

def power_law(n):
    return [max(1.0 - r / R, 0.0) ** (1.0 / n) for r in rs]

def defect_law(ustar):
    """u/Vbar = 1 + (u*/Vbar)(3.75 - 2.5 ln(R/y)),  y = R - r"""
    out = []
    for r in rs:
        y = max(R - r, 1e-9)
        out.append(1.0 + ustar * (3.75 - 2.5 * math.log(R / y)))
    return out

print(f"{'profile':<20}{'Vbar/Uc':>9}{'alpha':>9}{'beta':>8}")
vb, al_lam, be = ledger(poiseuille())
print(f"{'Poiseuille (laminar)':<20}{vb:9.4f}{al_lam:9.4f}{be:8.4f}")
for n in (6, 7, 8, 10):
    vb, al, be = ledger(power_law(n))
    exact = 2.0 * n * n / ((n + 1.0) * (2.0 * n + 1.0))
    print(f"{'power law n=' + str(n):<20}{vb:9.4f}{al:9.4f}{be:8.4f}   Vbar exact {exact:.4f}")

print()
cases = [("laminar", al_lam)]
print(f"{'Re':>8}{'f':>8}{'u*/Vbar':>9}{'Uc/Vbar':>9}{'alpha':>8}{'beta':>8}")
for Re in (1e4, 1e5, 1e6, 1e7):
    f = (0.790 * math.log(Re) - 1.64) ** -2.0        # Petukhov friction factor
    us = math.sqrt(f / 8.0)                          # u*/Vbar
    u = defect_law(us)
    vb, al, be = ledger(u)
    print(f"{Re:8.0e}{f:8.4f}{us:9.4f}{max(u) / vb:9.4f}{al:8.4f}{be:8.4f}")
    cases.append((f"turbulent Re={Re:.0e}", al))

print()
V, g = 3.0, 9.81                                     # 3 m/s pipe flow, velocity head
base = V * V / (2 * g)
for name, al in (cases[0], cases[1], cases[3]):
    print(f"{name:<21}alpha={al:6.4f}  alpha·V²/2g = {al * base:6.4f} m  "
          f"alpha=1 missing {al * base - base:6.4f} m ({(al - 1) * 100:6.2f}%)")
```

## The coordinates where the Reynolds number vanishes — the defect law

The second block of the table came from the velocity-defect law, not the power law. Write the log
law once at the centreline and once at an arbitrary point, subtract, and the viscosity drops out
entirely.

$$
\frac{U_c - \bar{u}}{u^*} = 2.5 \ln \frac{R}{y}
$$

Here $u^* = \sqrt{\tau_w/\rho}$ is the friction velocity. The right-hand side has no $\nu$ and no
$Re$ in it. Only the distance from the wall survives. Averaging over the section drops out the
constant $U_c/u^* - V/u^* = 3.75$, and $u^*/V = \sqrt{f/8}$ ties it to the friction factor.
Viscosity drops out of the subtraction. Only distance from the wall remains. The line folded above
is this constant 3.75.

## Putting $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in one table

| profile | $V/U_c$ | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| laminar parabola | 0.5000 | 2.0000 | 1.3333 | exact rationals |
| power law $n=7$ | 0.8167 | 1.0584 | 1.0204 | textbook default |
| power law $n=10$ | 0.8658 | 1.0307 | 1.0106 | high Reynolds |
| defect law $Re=10^4$ | — | 1.0835 | 1.0309 | $f = 0.0315$ |
| defect law $Re=10^7$ | — | 1.0227 | 1.0080 | $f = 0.0081$ |

Divide the four turbulent rows by $f$ and a pattern appears. $\alpha - 1$ is 2.7 to 2.8 times $f$,
and $\beta - 1$ is 0.98 times $f$. The two coefficients are pinned not to the profile shape but to
**the friction factor alone**. A rougher pipe has a larger $f$, and $\alpha$ grows with it. Run a
smooth pipe at high Reynolds and $\alpha$ falls to 1.02.

Split the $Re=10^6$ row. $\alpha-1 = 0.0322$ over $f = 0.0116$ is $0.0322/0.0116 \approx 2.78$. At $Re=10^4$, $0.0835/0.0315 \approx 2.65$. That is the 2.7 to 2.8 band. $\beta$ is tighter: $0.0114/0.0116 \approx 0.98$ at $Re=10^6$, and $0.0309/0.0315 \approx 0.98$ at $Re=10^4$.

At 3 m/s and $g=9.81$, the bare head is $V^2/2g = 9/19.62 = 0.4587$ m. Times 2 that is 0.9174 m, and the miss is the other 0.4587 m. Times 1.0322 it is 0.4735 m, a miss of 0.0148 m. At $Re=10^4$ the miss is 0.0383 m, 8.35 percent. Several metres of pipe loss hide 0.0148 m. They do not hide 0.4587 m.

The table also shows the power law and the defect law disagreeing slightly at the same Reynolds
number. The power law has an infinite slope at the wall and a kink at the centreline. Two
approximations differ by as much as they are approximations.

## Where this coefficient actually moves a number

The last block is the velocity head of a 3 m/s pipe flow. Setting $\alpha$ to 1 in turbulent flow
misses 0.0148 m at $Re=10^6$. If the head loss of that pipe run is several metres, ignore it. In
laminar flow it misses 0.4587 m. In the same calculation, that is 100%.

The number moves in a few specific places. Viscous fluids and narrow tubes are laminar, so $\alpha=2$.
In the entrance region the profile is still growing, and $\alpha$ sits somewhere between 1 and 2.
Outlet total pressure is the same choice: area average or mass-flux weight, which is $\int u^2$
versus $\int u^3$. When the two disagree, the solver is not wrong. The definition of the average is.

$\alpha$ is written like a constant, but the profile decides it. Pin it to 1 by hand and the laminar
ledger is off by half.

## The condition under which textbooks write $\alpha=1$ and move on

$\alpha=1$ holds only for a perfectly flat profile. The approximation works in turbulent flow not
because turbulence is flat but because $f$ is small. With $f$ around 0.01, $\alpha$ is 1.03, and 3%
disappears into the other uncertainties of a piping calculation.

So the test is not "is it turbulent" but "how large is $f$". How transition stacks those layers
inside a pipe is in
[the critical Reynolds number and pipe transition](/en/posts/2026-05-05-reynolds-pipe-transition-turbulence/).
A rough pipe, a low Reynolds number, laminar flow — any one of those and $\alpha$ gets integrated
again. The computation is one integral, as in the code above.
