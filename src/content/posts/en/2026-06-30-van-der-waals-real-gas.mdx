## Nitrogen at 100 bar is heavier than an ideal gas

$Pv = RT$ treats molecules as points and drops the forces between them. It holds when the gas is dilute. The molecules sit far apart.

Raise the pressure and the gaps shrink. A weak attraction (the van der Waals force) reduces the volume. The molecules' own volume remains.

The two effects oppose each other. Attraction shrinks volume. Finite size inflates it. Temperature and pressure decide the winner. So a real gas can be heavier or lighter than an ideal gas, and the ranking flips with the conditions. When that density ranking flips, [the threshold where light fluid on the bottom turns convection on](/en/posts/2026-07-09-rayleigh-benard-convection-lorenz/) moves with it.

## The compressibility factor Z — one number for the mismatch

Bundled into one number, the mismatch is the compressibility factor.

$$
Z = \frac{Pv}{RT} = \frac{v_\text{actual}}{v_\text{ideal}}
$$

$P$ is pressure, $v$ molar volume, $R$ the gas constant, and $T$ temperature. $Z=1$ is an ideal gas. Farther from 1 means a larger miss.

$Z<1$ means attraction wins. The real volume is smaller than the ideal one. $Z>1$ means finite volume wins. Molecules push the volume up. In the nitrogen example, $Z \approx 0.79$ means the real density is $1/0.79 \approx 1.27$ times the ideal value.

## The principle of corresponding states: where every gas looks alike

Each gas has its own $Z$ curve. Divide pressure and temperature by that gas's critical values and the curves nearly stack.

$$
P_R = \frac{P}{P_c}, \qquad T_R = \frac{T}{T_c}
$$

$P_c$ and $T_c$ are the critical pressure and temperature. The **principle of corresponding states** says that equal reduced pressure $P_R$ and reduced temperature $T_R$ give nearly the same $Z$.

Nitrogen, methane, or carbon dioxide: at $T_R=1.5$ and $P_R=2$ the $Z$ values sit close together. The position relative to the critical point sets the behavior, not the name of the molecule. One generalized chart covers a lot of gases.

The nitrogen in the code sits in a different cell from that example. $T = 200$ and $T_c = 126.2$, so $T_R = 200/126.2 \approx 1.585$. $P = 10^7$ and $P_c = 3.39\times 10^6$, so $P_R = 10/3.39 \approx 2.95$. Turning the printed $Z = 0.7905$ back into a reduced volume gives $v_R = Z\,(T/T_c)\,(P_c/P)\,(8/3) \approx 1.13$. Put that point back on the reduced isotherm and $P_R$ returns as 2.95. The printed $Z$ is a point on that supercritical isotherm.

## van der Waals: mimicking molecules with two constants

In 1873 van der Waals put two corrections on the ideal gas.

$$
\left(P + \frac{a}{v^2}\right)(v - b) = RT
$$

$a/v^2$ is the pressure the attraction removes. It grows as the volume shrinks. $b$ is the volume the molecules occupy, so the free space is $v-b$.

The two constants are fixed at the critical point. There the $P$–$v$ isotherm has an inflection, and the slope and curvature are both zero.

$$
\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial v}\right)_{T_c} = 0, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial v^2}\right)_{T_c} = 0
$$

Solving those conditions gives the constants and the critical compressibility.

$$
a = \frac{27 R^2 T_c^2}{64 P_c}, \qquad b = \frac{R T_c}{8 P_c}, \qquad Z_c = \frac{P_c v_c}{R T_c} = \frac{3}{8}
$$

$Z_c = 3/8 = 0.375$ does not depend on which gas. Real gases have $Z_c$ from 0.27 to 0.29. 0.375 is about one third above a value near 0.28. Quantitatively high. The picture of the loop and the critical point is right.

In reduced variables, $a$ and $b$ drop out.

$$
P_R = \frac{8 T_R}{3 v_R - 1} - \frac{3}{v_R^2}
$$

That line is the equation form of corresponding states. The critical point is fixed at $(v_R, P_R, T_R) = (1, 1, 1)$. The denominator $3v_R - 1$ is zero at $v_R = 1/3$. To the left of that, the volume is smaller than the molecular volume $b$, and the isotherm is not drawn.

## The S-curve below the critical point and the Maxwell construction

For $T_R > 1$ the isotherm falls monotonically. Raise the pressure and the volume drops.

For $T_R < 1$ an S-shaped loop appears. The middle has $(\partial P/\partial v)_T > 0$. Pressure up, volume up. Mechanically unstable. A real substance skips that interval.

A single horizontal line joins liquid and vapor instead. Its height is the saturation pressure. The **Maxwell equal-area construction** picks the height where the areas cut above and below the line are equal. That is the same statement as equal Gibbs free energy for liquid and vapor. Only one pressure lets the two phases coexist.

## Solving for Z in Python

Rearranged for $Z$, van der Waals is a cubic. With the critical constants, any $P$ and $T$ has a $Z$.

```python
import numpy as np

R = 8.314  # J/(mol·K)

def vdw_constants(Tc, Pc):
    """van der Waals a, b from the critical constants."""
    a = 27.0 * R**2 * Tc**2 / (64.0 * Pc)
    b = R * Tc / (8.0 * Pc)
    return a, b

def vdw_compressibility(P, T, Tc, Pc):
    """Physical root of Z^3 - (1+B)Z^2 + A Z - A B = 0."""
    a, b = vdw_constants(Tc, Pc)
    A = a * P / (R * T)**2          # dimensionless size of the attraction term
    B = b * P / (R * T)            # dimensionless size of the finite-volume term
    coeffs = [1.0, -(1.0 + B), A, -A * B]
    roots = np.roots(coeffs)
    real = roots[np.abs(roots.imag) < 1e-8].real
    real = real[real > B]          # only v > b  ⇒  Z > B is physical
    return real.max(), real.min(), real.size

# Nitrogen: Tc = 126.2 K, Pc = 3.39 MPa, M = 28 g/mol
Tc, Pc, M = 126.2, 3.39e6, 0.028
P, T = 1.0e7, 200.0                # 100 bar, 200 K
Z_gas, Z_liq, n = vdw_compressibility(P, T, Tc, Pc)

rho_ideal = P * M / (R * T)        # ideal-gas density
rho_real = rho_ideal / Z_gas       # real density
print(f"roots = {n},  Z = {Z_gas:.4f}")
print(f"ideal density = {rho_ideal:6.1f} kg/m^3")
print(f"real  density = {rho_real:6.1f} kg/m^3  ({100*(rho_real/rho_ideal-1):+.0f}%)")
```

Three lines come back.

```text
roots = 1,  Z = 0.7905
ideal density =  168.4 kg/m^3
real  density =  213.0 kg/m^3  (+27%)
```

$213.0/168.4 = 1.265$. The increase is 26.5 percent, and the printed $+27\%$ is that value rounded to an integer. $T_R \approx 1.59$, so the state is supercritical. One real root means no liquid-vapor split. The ideal-gas density is still about a quarter lighter than the real one.

## Supercritical injection and CFD — why the equation of state shakes the grid

A liquid-rocket chamber usually runs above the propellant's critical pressure. LOX (liquid oxygen) or cryogenic nitrogen entering that chamber does not form the familiar droplets.

Surface tension is gone. Past the critical point the liquid-vapor boundary collapses. The jet does not break into drops. Density gradients fray like a comb and mix with the surroundings. Supercritical, or transcritical, injection.

The equation of state shakes the grid because density is tied to pressure and temperature nonlinearly. An ideal-gas solver misses the jet density by tens of percent, and the momentum flux $\rho u^2$ and the mixing length move with it. Production codes use a cubic EOS (Peng–Robinson, SRK) or NASG (Noble–Abel Stiffened Gas). $Z$ is the quantity that model solves in every cell.

## Writing a 27 percent density error as a 27 percent momentum error is a failure

At the same speed, $\rho u^2$ scales with the square of the density ratio. $1.265^2 \approx 1.60$, so a 26.5 percent density error is about 60 percent in the momentum flux. Writing "density is 27 percent off, so the flux is 27 percent off" drops the square.
