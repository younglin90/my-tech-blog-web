## A number I never supplied came out as 1.0000

I put one sinusoidal shear wave and one sinusoidal temperature wave on a D2Q9 lattice and measured
the kinematic viscosity $\nu$ and the thermal diffusivity $\alpha$ from how fast each amplitude
decayed. Then I repeated the measurement with the relaxation time $\tau$ doubled step by step:
0.6, 0.8, 1.2. All three runs returned a Prandtl number (the ratio of momentum diffusion to
thermal diffusion) of 1.0001, 1.0000, 1.0001.

I never supplied that number as a material property. The lattice picked it.

This post points at the line of code where the lock sits, then re-measures on the same lattice what
opens up once energy gets its own distribution function. And $\mathrm{Pr}$ is not the only constant
locked in. The specific heat ratio $\gamma$ is locked too, and on D2Q9 its value is 2 — not the 1.4
of air. Even after the lock comes off, $\tau_g$ cannot be pushed indefinitely, so I measured where
it breaks down as well.

## One τ gets used in two places

Here is the lattice Boltzmann equation with a BGK collision term.

$$
f_i(\boldsymbol{x} + \boldsymbol{e}_i \Delta t,\; t + \Delta t) - f_i(\boldsymbol{x}, t)
= -\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left[ f_i - f_i^{\mathrm{eq}} \right]
$$

$f_i$ is the distribution function along lattice direction $i$, $\boldsymbol{e}_i$ is the discrete
velocity in that direction, and $\tau$ is the relaxation time. Carry the Chapman–Enskog expansion
(an expansion that treats the departure from equilibrium as a small parameter and separates the
result order by order) to first order, and the viscous stress falls out of the first-order
non-equilibrium part of $f$. That gives the familiar result.

$$
\nu = c_s^{2}\left(\tau - \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)
$$

$c_s$ is the lattice speed of sound and $\Delta t/2$ is the correction left behind by
discretization. Where that half step comes from is worked out separately in
[The Δt/2 the LBM Discretization Left Behind](/en/posts/2026-08-26-lbm-trapezoidal-integration-half-timestep/).

The trouble starts right after. Define internal energy as a second moment of that same $f$, and the
temperature equation drops out of the same expansion through the same first-order non-equilibrium
term. The thermal diffusivity comes out with an identical shape.

$$
\alpha = c_s^{2}\left(\tau - \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right)
$$

The right-hand sides match character for character. Only one conclusion follows.

$$
\mathrm{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha} = 1
$$

The reason fits in one line. Momentum flux and heat flux both come from the same non-equilibrium
term of the same distribution function, and there is exactly one time constant sitting in front of
that term. One constant means the ratio is not yours to choose.

That $\mathrm{Pr}$ sits near 1 for gases is an experimental fact, and the
[Reynolds analogy](/en/posts/2026-06-23-reynolds-analogy-friction-heat/) that links friction to heat
transfer follows from it. But there it is an approximation and a modeling choice. Here it is
enforced. Feed the solver water or liquid metal and it still returns 1.

## Give energy its own distribution function

The fix is structurally simple. The problem came from having only one constant, so make a second
one. Let $f$ carry mass and momentum only, and hand energy to a second distribution function $g_i$.
That is the double-distribution-function (DDF) method.

The moment conditions $g$ must satisfy take two lines.

$$
\sum_i g_i = \rho E, \qquad
\sum_i \boldsymbol{e}_i\, g_i = \rho E \boldsymbol{u} + p\,\boldsymbol{u}
$$

$E = c_v T + |\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}/2$ is the total energy per unit mass and $p\boldsymbol{u}$ is the
work done by pressure. The $p\boldsymbol{u}$ in the second line is the crux. The convective flux of
energy is not $\rho E \boldsymbol{u}$ alone but $\rho H \boldsymbol{u}$, pressure work included, so
the equilibrium distribution for $g$ cannot simply copy the one for $f$.

Relax $g$ with $\tau_g$, run the same Chapman–Enskog expansion, and the thermal diffusivity now
looks at $\tau_g$.

$$
\mathrm{Pr} = \frac{\nu}{\alpha}
= \frac{\tau_f - \Delta t/2}{\tau_g - \Delta t/2}
$$

The Reynolds number sets $\tau_f$; the Prandtl number sets $\tau_g$. The two demands finally hold
separate knobs.

## Measuring the lock and its release on one lattice in Python

Rather than leave it as an argument, I measured it. I set up $f$ on D2Q9 and $g$ on D2Q5 (a
five-velocity lattice for temperature only), with a sine wave varying along $y$ as the initial
condition. The shear wave amplitude decays as $\exp(-\nu k^{2} t)$ and the temperature wave as
$\exp(-\alpha k^{2} t)$. A least-squares fit to the log amplitude returns $\nu$ and $\alpha$.

```python
import math

NY, CS2 = 64, 1.0 / 3.0
K = 2.0 * math.pi / NY

EX9 = [0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, -1, -1, 1]
EY9 = [0, 0, 1, 0, -1, 1, 1, -1, -1]
W9 = [4/9, 1/9, 1/9, 1/9, 1/9, 1/36, 1/36, 1/36, 1/36]

EY5 = [0, 0, 1, 0, -1]
W5 = [1/3, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6, 1/6]


def feq_d2q9(rho, ux, uy):
    u2 = ux * ux + uy * uy
    return [w * rho * (1 + 3 * (ex * ux + ey * uy)
                       + 4.5 * (ex * ux + ey * uy) ** 2 - 1.5 * u2)
            for w, ex, ey in zip(W9, EX9, EY9)]


def geq_d2q5(temp):
    return [w * temp for w in W5]


def fit_decay_rate(samples):
    """samples = [(step, amplitude)] -> least-squares slope of ln(amplitude)."""
    n = len(samples)
    xs = [s for s, _ in samples]
    ys = [math.log(a) for _, a in samples]
    mx, my = sum(xs) / n, sum(ys) / n
    num = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys))
    den = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
    return -num / den


def run_shear_wave(tau_f, steps=3000, amp=1e-3):
    f = [[0.0] * NY for _ in range(9)]
    for y in range(NY):
        for i, v in enumerate(feq_d2q9(1.0, amp * math.sin(K * y), 0.0)):
            f[i][y] = v
    log = []
    for step in range(steps + 1):
        rho = [sum(f[i][y] for i in range(9)) for y in range(NY)]
        ux = [sum(f[i][y] * EX9[i] for i in range(9)) / rho[y] for y in range(NY)]
        if step % 200 == 0:
            a = 2.0 / NY * sum(ux[y] * math.sin(K * y) for y in range(NY))
            log.append((step, a))
        post = [[0.0] * NY for _ in range(9)]
        for y in range(NY):
            eq = feq_d2q9(rho[y], ux[y], 0.0)
            for i in range(9):
                post[i][y] = f[i][y] - (f[i][y] - eq[i]) / tau_f
        for i in range(9):
            for y in range(NY):
                f[i][(y + EY9[i]) % NY] = post[i][y]
    return fit_decay_rate(log) / (K * K)


def run_thermal_wave(tau_g, amp=1e-3):
    """Pick the step count from tau_g so the amplitude always decays by e^-2.5."""
    alpha_th = CS2 * (tau_g - 0.5)
    steps = max(240, min(12000, int(2.5 / (alpha_th * K * K))))
    every = max(1, steps // 12)
    g = [[0.0] * NY for _ in range(5)]
    for y in range(NY):
        for i, v in enumerate(geq_d2q5(amp * math.sin(K * y))):
            g[i][y] = v
    log = []
    for step in range(steps + 1):
        temp = [sum(g[i][y] for i in range(5)) for y in range(NY)]
        if step % every == 0:
            a = 2.0 / NY * sum(temp[y] * math.sin(K * y) for y in range(NY))
            log.append((step, a))
        post = [[0.0] * NY for _ in range(5)]
        for y in range(NY):
            eq = geq_d2q5(temp[y])
            for i in range(5):
                post[i][y] = g[i][y] - (g[i][y] - eq[i]) / tau_g
        for i in range(5):
            for y in range(NY):
                g[i][(y + EY5[i]) % NY] = post[i][y]
    return fit_decay_rate(log) / (K * K)


def tau_for(nu, target_pr):
    return 0.5 + nu / (target_pr * CS2)


TAU_F = 0.8
nu = run_shear_wave(TAU_F)
print(f"tau_f = {TAU_F}   nu(theory) = {CS2*(TAU_F-0.5):.6f}   nu(measured) = {nu:.6f}")
print()
print("[A] single distribution: one tau relaxes momentum AND energy")
print(f"{'tau':>6} {'nu':>10} {'alpha':>10} {'Pr':>8}")
for t in (0.6, 0.8, 1.2):
    n_, a_ = run_shear_wave(t), run_thermal_wave(t)
    print(f"{t:6.2f} {n_:10.6f} {a_:10.6f} {n_/a_:8.4f}")
print()
print("[B] double distribution: tau_g chosen for a target Pr (tau_f = 0.8)")
print(f"{'gas':>8} {'Pr(target)':>11} {'tau_g':>8} {'alpha':>10} {'Pr(meas)':>9} {'err%':>7}")
for name, pr in (("mercury", 0.025), ("air", 0.71), ("Pr=1", 1.0), ("water", 7.0)):
    tg = tau_for(nu, pr)
    a_ = run_thermal_wave(tg)
    prm = nu / a_
    print(f"{name:>8} {pr:11.3f} {tg:8.4f} {a_:10.6f} {prm:9.4f} {100*(prm-pr)/pr:7.2f}")

print()
print("[C] how far can tau_g be pushed?  (theory: alpha = cs2*(tau_g-0.5))")
print(f"{'tau_g':>7} {'alpha(th)':>10} {'alpha(meas)':>12} {'err%':>7}")
for tg in (0.51, 0.55, 0.7, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0, 12.5):
    th = CS2 * (tg - 0.5)
    ms = run_thermal_wave(tg)
    print(f"{tg:7.2f} {th:10.5f} {ms:12.5f} {100*(ms-th)/th:7.2f}")
```

## There is a second locked constant — the specific heat ratio

Stop at $\mathrm{Pr}$ and you miss the second lock. A particle on the lattice moves along $D$
translational directions and nothing else. No rotation, no vibration. The heat capacity at constant
volume then counts translational degrees of freedom only, giving $c_v = DR/2$, and the gas constant
comes back as $R = c_v / (D/2)$. The specific heat ratio is decided for you.

$$
c_v = \frac{(D + n_0)\,R}{2}, \qquad
\gamma = 1 + \frac{2}{D + n_0}
$$

$n_0$ is the number of internal degrees of freedom carried on top of the translational ones. Do
nothing and $n_0 = 0$.

| Lattice | $n_0$ | $c_v$ | $\gamma$ | Matching gas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D2Q9 | 0 | $1.0R$ | 2.000 | none |
| D3Q19 | 0 | $1.5R$ | 1.667 | monatomic (Ar, He) |
| D2Q9 | 3 | $2.5R$ | 1.400 | air |
| D3Q19 | 2 | $2.5R$ | 1.400 | air |
| D2Q9 | 4 | $3.0R$ | 1.333 | water vapor |

In two dimensions, an untouched lattice has $\gamma = 2$. Since the speed of sound is
$\sqrt{\gamma R T}$, that is $\sqrt{2/1.4} = 1.195$ times the value for air — 19.5% too fast. Mach
number, shock angles, and nozzle choking conditions all shift by the same factor. In compressible
simulations this bites before $\mathrm{Pr}$ does.

dimensions and 3 in two.

## How far τ_g can be pushed

Table [C] measures how long $\alpha = c_s^{2}(\tau_g - \Delta t/2)$ stays trustworthy. Out to
$\tau_g = 2$ the error stays under 0.8%. At 4 it is 4.6%, at 8 it is 22%, and at 12.5 the gap opens
to 49%. The measurement returns barely half the theoretical value.

The reason lies in where that formula came from. $\alpha = c_s^{2}(\tau_g - \Delta t/2)$ is a
first-order result of the Chapman–Enskog expansion. For the expansion to hold, the relaxation time
must be short compared with the time scale of the flow. As $\tau_g$ grows, the discarded
second-order term — a hyperdiffusive term proportional to the fourth power of the wavenumber —
grows to the size of the first-order one. The practical ceiling is around $\alpha \approx 0.5$ in
lattice units.

That is why the mercury row in [B] broke. Getting $\mathrm{Pr} = 0.025$ at $\tau_f = 0.8$ demands
$\alpha = 4.0$, eight times that ceiling. The prescription is not to raise $\tau_g$ further but to
lower $\nu$. Holding $\alpha \le 0.5$ while reaching $\mathrm{Pr} = 0.025$ requires
$\nu \le 0.0125$, that is $\tau_f \le 0.5375$. Now the opposite wall shows up: BGK turns unstable
as $\tau_f$ approaches 0.5.

So the picture is this. High $\mathrm{Pr}$ runs into a stability wall on the $\tau_g \to 0.5$ side,
and low $\mathrm{Pr}$ runs into an accuracy wall as $\tau_g$ grows. What DDF hands you is a window,
not unlimited freedom.

## Which ledger does viscous heating go on

Splitting $f$ and $g$ creates a new problem. The energy equation contains the viscous dissipation
term $\boldsymbol{u} \cdot (\nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{\Pi})$. But $\boldsymbol{\Pi}$ is a quantity
that emerges from the first-order non-equilibrium part of $f$, and it relaxes with $\tau_f$.

$$
\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(1)} = -2\rho\, c_s^{2}\left(\tau_f - \frac{\Delta t}{2}\right) \boldsymbol{S}
$$

$\boldsymbol{S}$ is the strain rate tensor. Since $g$ relaxes with $\tau_g$, the dissipation term
returned by the expansion of $g$ carries $\tau_g$ in front of it. The moment the two relaxation
times differ, the coefficients disagree. They match on their own only when $\mathrm{Pr} = 1$. That
is why coupled DDF schemes attach one more correction term to the $g$ equation.

Computing that correction locally at each lattice node requires closing $\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(1)}$ in
terms of moments. Grad's 13-moment approximation fills that slot.

$$
f_i \simeq w_i\left[\rho
+ \frac{\rho\, \boldsymbol{e}_i \cdot \boldsymbol{u}}{c_s^{2}}
+ \frac{(\boldsymbol{e}_i \boldsymbol{e}_i - c_s^{2}\boldsymbol{I}) :
\left(\rho \boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(1)}\right)}{2 c_s^{4}}\right]
$$

Set $\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(1)} = 0$ and the familiar equilibrium distribution comes back untouched.
Where that polynomial comes from is written up in
[Maxwell–Boltzmann Inside Nine Arrows](/en/posts/2026-05-25-lbm-equilibrium-hermite-d2q9/). Grad's
approximation carries the same expansion one order further, feeding the non-equilibrium stress back
into the distribution function itself. This is also the fork between the coupled DDF papers of 2007
(the Li et al. model for compressible Navier–Stokes) and the low-Mach decoupled models. The former
attaches the correction explicitly; the latter throws viscous heating away entirely.

## How much physics one distribution function can carry

A single $f$ can carry $\rho$, $\boldsymbol{u}$, and one relaxation time — that is the budget. Put
temperature on a second moment of that same $f$ and $\mathrm{Pr}$ and $\gamma$ stop being fluid
properties and become lattice constants. Low-Mach Boussinesq simulations never expose the problem,
since temperature there is a passive scalar anyway. Move to compressible thermal flow and both
constants come due at once.

In a thermal LBM code you inherited, three lines are enough to find first. Does temperature come
out of a moment of $f$, or out of a separate array? Is $\gamma$ hardcoded as a constant, or computed
from $D + n_0$? And is there a correction term next to the collision operator of $g$ that uses
$\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{(1)}$? If the third one is missing while $\tau_f \ne \tau_g$, the viscous heating
in that code is a quantity nobody ever computed.
