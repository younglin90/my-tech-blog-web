## Pr changed, so why didn't the temperature?

Picture a Couette channel. The bottom wall is at rest and the top wall slides at speed $U$. Both walls have the same temperature. The middle still heats up, because viscosity turns kinetic energy into heat (viscous heating). The peak temperature rise is exactly proportional to the Prandtl number (Pr, the ratio of momentum diffusion to heat diffusion).

This post is about changing Pr in a lattice Boltzmann (LB) thermal model. Split the distribution into two sets, give each its own relaxation time, and Pr looks easy to tune. Run it, though, and conduction changes while viscous heating stays frozen at its Pr = 1 value. You'll see why, and you'll get the one-line fix: the quasi-equilibrium $g^*$.

Try it in the simulation below. It kicks a single point out of equilibrium once, then shows how much of each moment survives every collision step.

## One set of populations only knows Pr = 1

The BGK collision operator pulls every non-equilibrium moment toward equilibrium at one rate $\omega$. Stress and heat flux relax on the same clock, so viscosity and conductivity are tied to the same $\omega$. That is why the continuous BGK equation gives Pr = 1.

Air has Pr = 0.71 and water about 7. A model locked to Pr = 1 can't even get the ratio of boundary-layer thicknesses right. There are two remedies. One relaxes only the heat-flux moment at a different rate inside the collision operator (MRT, Shakhov-type). The other hands energy to a separate distribution. This post follows the second route.

## Two sets: f carries mass and momentum, g carries total energy

In a two-population model, $f_i$ carries density and momentum, and $g_i$ carries the total energy $E=\rho(c_vT+u^2/2)$ ($i=0,\dots,Q-1$). The kinetic equations are:

$$
f_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_i,\,t+1)=f_i+\omega_\mu\,(f_i^{eq}-f_i)
$$

$$
g_i(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}_i,\,t+1)=g_i+\omega_\mu\,(g_i^{eq}-g_i)+(\omega_\kappa-\omega_\mu)\,(g_i^{*}-g_i)
$$

$\omega_\mu$ sets viscosity (kinematic viscosity $\nu$), and $\omega_\kappa$ sets conduction (thermal diffusivity $\alpha$). $g_i^{eq}$ is the local equilibrium and $g_i^*$ is the quasi-equilibrium.

The two rates map to the diffusivities as:

$$
\nu=c_s^2\left(\frac{1}{\omega_\mu}-\frac12\right),\qquad
\alpha=c_s^2\left(\frac{1}{\omega_\kappa}-\frac12\right),\qquad
\mathrm{Pr}=\frac{\nu}{\alpha}
$$

$c_s^2=1/3$ is the squared lattice sound speed. With $\tau_\mu=1/\omega_\mu$ and $\tau_\kappa=1/\omega_\kappa$, $\mathrm{Pr}=(\tau_\mu-\tfrac12)/(\tau_\kappa-\tfrac12)$.

## Viscous work hides inside the energy flux

In this toy, $g^{eq}$ fixes only three moments. Zeroth: $E$. First (energy flux): $(E+p)\mathbf{u}$. Second: $c_s^2E\,\mathbf{I}+E\,\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}$. The non-equilibrium part of the first moment, $\mathbf{Q}$, is the actual heat flux.

At first order in the Chapman–Enskog expansion (solving for non-equilibrium at small Knudsen number), $\mathbf{Q}\approx-\tau\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf{R}^{eq}$. Here $\nabla(c_s^2E)$ splits into two pieces:

$$
\nabla(c_s^2E)=c_s^2\rho c_v\nabla T+c_s^2\rho\,(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\!\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\dots
$$

The first term is Fourier conduction. The second is the raw material for the viscous work $-\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}$. It has exactly the shape of the non-equilibrium stress in $f$, $\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{neq}=\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}^{eq}\approx-\tau_\mu\rho c_s^2(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^T)$. The problem is the $\tau$ in front.

If $g$ is pulled only toward equilibrium at $\omega_\kappa$, both pieces come out carrying $\tau_\kappa$. The viscous-work coefficient scales with $\alpha$ instead of $\nu$. Conduction is right, but viscous heating stays at its Pr = 1 value. That's the red curve in the first viz.

## Quasi-equilibrium g*: tie only the flux to the stress clock

The fix is one line in the definition of $g^*$. It shares every moment with $g^{eq}$ except the energy flux:

$$
\mathbf{q}^{*}=\mathbf{q}^{eq}+\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}^{eq})
$$

$\mathbf{P}$ is the actual momentum flux measured from $f$, and $\mathbf{P}^{eq}$ is its equilibrium value.

Taking the first moment of the kinetic equation gives a one-step update for the two non-equilibrium moments:

$$
\mathbf{Q}'=(1-\omega_\kappa)\,\mathbf{Q}+(\omega_\kappa-\omega_\mu)\,\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{neq},\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Pi}'^{neq}=(1-\omega_\mu)\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{neq}
$$

$\mathbf{Q}$ itself relaxes at $\omega_\kappa$, so conduction follows $\alpha$. At the same time, if $\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}^{neq}$, then $\mathbf{Q}'=\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Pi}'^{neq}$ holds at the next step. The viscous-work piece rides the stress clock $\omega_\mu$. A Chapman–Enskog calculation says the same thing: the viscous work carrying $\tau_\kappa$ cancels against the $(\omega_\kappa-\omega_\mu)$ term, leaving only $\tau_\mu$.

With $\omega_\kappa=\omega_\mu$ the $g^*$ term vanishes. The model reduces to single relaxation, and Pr = 1. $g^*$ is a correction that only works when Pr $\ne 1$. The limit that higher moments of a forcing term must respect was covered in [the line a force term has to respect](/en/posts/2026-09-30-lbm-forcing-term-third-moment-limit/). The question here is the same: which moment relaxes at which rate.

## Code: one Couette channel, two kinds of g

The analytical solution is simple. The steady energy equation is $kT''+\mu(u')^2=0$. With a linear velocity profile, temperature is a parabola:

$$
\theta(y)\equiv\frac{T-T_0}{U^2/(8c_v)}=4\,\mathrm{Pr}\,\eta(1-\eta),\qquad \eta=\frac{y}{H}
$$

The peak in the middle is $\theta_{max}=\mathrm{Pr}$. The code below runs D2Q9 with a single periodic cell in $x$ and solves only along $y$. Walls use Guo's non-equilibrium extrapolation. For simplicity, $f$ uses an isothermal equilibrium and energy is coupled one way. In low-Mach ($U=0.1$) Couette flow the pressure is uniform, so this simplification doesn't change the result.

```python
import numpy as np

# D2Q9, one periodic cell in x → 1D arrays along y
cx = np.array([0, 1, 0, -1, 0, 1, -1, -1, 1])
cy = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, -1, 1, 1, -1, -1])
w = np.array([4/9] + [1/9]*4 + [1/36]*4)
CV, T0 = 1.0, 1.0

def feq_lattice(rho, ux):
    cu = cx[:, None]*ux
    return w[:, None]*rho*(1 + 3*cu + 4.5*cu**2 - 1.5*ux**2)

def geq_energy(E, rho, ux):
    p = rho*(E/rho - 0.5*ux**2)/CV          # p = rho*T
    qx = (E + p)*ux
    cu = cx[:, None]*ux
    return w[:, None]*(E + 3*cx[:, None]*qx + 4.5*E*cu**2 - 1.5*E*ux**2)

def couette_heating(Pr, quasi, N=21, U=0.1, tau_mu=1.0, steps=8000):
    om_mu = 1/tau_mu
    om_ka = 1/(0.5 + (tau_mu - 0.5)/Pr)
    rho = np.ones(N); ux = np.zeros(N)
    E = rho*CV*T0*np.ones(N)
    f = feq_lattice(rho, ux); g = geq_energy(E, rho, ux)
    uw = np.zeros(N); uw[-1] = U
    for _ in range(steps):
        rho = f.sum(0); ux = (cx[:, None]*f).sum(0)/rho; E = g.sum(0)
        fe = feq_lattice(rho, ux); ge = geq_energy(E, rho, ux)
        Pxy = ((cx*cy)[:, None]*(f - fe)).sum(0)
        f = f + om_mu*(fe - f)
        if quasi:   # g*: quasi-equilibrium with u·(P - P_eq) added to the energy flux
            gs = ge + w[:, None]*3*cy[:, None]*(ux*Pxy)
            g = g + om_mu*(ge - g) + (om_ka - om_mu)*(gs - g)
        else:       # plain two-τ: g relaxes only to equilibrium at ω_κ
            g = g + om_ka*(ge - g)
        for k in range(9):
            f[k] = np.roll(f[k], cy[k]); g[k] = np.roll(g[k], cy[k])
        for wall, nb in ((0, 1), (N-1, N-2)):   # non-equilibrium extrapolation walls
            r = f[:, nb].sum(); un = (cx*f[:, nb]).sum()/r; En = g[:, nb].sum()
            fn = feq_lattice(np.array([r]), np.array([un]))[:, 0]
            gn = geq_energy(np.array([En]), np.array([r]), np.array([un]))[:, 0]
            Ew = r*(CV*T0 + 0.5*uw[wall]**2)
            f[:, wall] = feq_lattice(np.array([r]), np.array([uw[wall]]))[:, 0] + f[:, nb] - fn
            g[:, wall] = geq_energy(np.array([Ew]), np.array([r]), np.array([uw[wall]]))[:, 0] + g[:, nb] - gn
    rho = f.sum(0); ux = (cx[:, None]*f).sum(0)/rho
    T = (g.sum(0)/rho - 0.5*ux**2)/CV
    theta = (T - T0)/(U**2/(8*CV))
    return theta.max(), ux

print(" Pr   exact   2-tau(g_eq)   g* quasi-eq")
for Pr in (0.5, 0.71, 1.0, 2.0):
    a, _ = couette_heating(Pr, False)
    b, u = couette_heating(Pr, True)
    print(f"{Pr:4.2f}  {Pr:6.3f}   {a:9.3f}    {b:9.3f}")
print(f"u(mid)/U = {u[10]/0.1:.4f}")
```

```text
0.71   0.710       1.000        0.710
1.00   1.000       1.000        1.000
2.00   2.000       1.000        1.997

```

## What the 1.000 is telling you

The plain two-τ column reads 1.000 regardless of Pr. That isn't noise, it's structure. The viscous-work coefficient scales with $\tau_\kappa-\tfrac12$, and so does the conductivity. In their ratio, $\theta_{max}$, $\tau_\kappa$ cancels exactly. For air (Pr = 0.71) that overpredicts the temperature rise between the walls by 41%. For a Pr = 7 fluid like water it underpredicts by a factor of 7.

The $g^*$ column reads 0.498, 0.710, 1.000, 1.997. On 21 lattice nodes that's within 0.4% of the analytical solution. The leftover comes from the wall treatment and resolution. The midline velocity $u/U=0.5000$ is identical because both models share the same $f$. The viscous-heating error is invisible in the velocity field. Unless you compare the temperature field against an analytical solution, you'll miss it.

## Go compressible and f gets a quasi-equilibrium too

The $f$ in this post used an isothermal equilibrium. In compressible two-population models, $f$ also gets a quasi-equilibrium $f^*$. D2Q9's third moments have a defect: $c_{ix}^3=c_{ix}$. When temperature varies or Mach number grows, that defect puts an error term into the stress. It's the same defect seen in [the three Hermite roots that fixed D2Q9](/en/posts/2026-10-05-gauss-hermite-quadrature-lattice-velocities/). $f^*$ carries a pressure tensor that corrects it. $f^*$ repairs the momentum flux; $g^*$ attaches viscous work to the energy flux.

The principle is the same. Equilibrium and quasi-equilibrium share the conserved quantities (density, momentum, total energy). The only difference is the one moment that needs its own relaxation rate. Deciding where to put the pressure term for a non-ideal fluid, in [two forms of non-ideal LBM forcing](/en/posts/2026-08-21-nonideal-lbm-pressure-vs-freeenergy-forcing/), was ultimately the same question.

## The one line to check when your thermal LB code sets τκ separately

Open the collision step for the energy distribution and look at where $\omega_\kappa$ multiplies. If all of $g$ is pulled toward equilibrium at $\omega_\kappa$, viscous heating is stuck at its Pr = 1 value. One Couette run is enough to check. If $\theta_{max}$ doesn't track Pr, the quasi-equilibrium term is missing.

Conversely, if you've only validated at $\omega_\kappa=\omega_\mu$, this bug stays hidden forever. Put at least one viscous-heating case with Pr $\ne 1$ in your thermal LB validation suite. A conduction-only test (temperature diffusing in a fluid at rest) can't exercise this term.
