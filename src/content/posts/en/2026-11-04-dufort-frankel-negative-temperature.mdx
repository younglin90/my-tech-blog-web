## A 20x larger Δt, and the middle of the bar dropped below zero

Take a bar with both ends held at zero and start it with a $\sin x$ temperature profile. This is the heat equation, so the answer is known. The shape stays the same and the amplitude decays as $e^{-\alpha t}$. Nowhere can the temperature go below zero.

FTCS (forward-time, central-space explicit) blows up once $r=\alpha\Delta t/\Delta x^2$ exceeds 0.5. So I switched to DuFort–Frankel, the "unconditionally stable explicit scheme." I raised $r$ from 0.5 to 10, a 20x larger time step. Nothing blew up. But at $t=1.48$ the center temperature came out as $-0.83$. The exact value is $+0.052$.

This post traces where that negative number comes from. Short version: at large $\Delta t$, DuFort–Frankel does not solve the heat equation. It solves a damped wave equation. By the end you can compute the scheme's real $\Delta t$ limit in one line.

## Three schemes, one bar

The setup comes from homework HW#2 in Prof. Moon-Sang Kim's CFD course: 21 grid points, $\alpha=2$, initial condition $\sin x$.

$$
u_i^{n+1}=u_i^n+r\left(u_{i+1}^n-2u_i^n+u_{i-1}^n\right)
$$

That is FTCS. $u_i^n$ is the temperature at node $i$, time level $n$. It is the simplest option and is stable only for $r\le 1/2$.

DuFort–Frankel starts from leapfrog (a central time difference over two levels). Leapfrog applied to diffusion is unstable for every $r$. The fix replaces the center value $u_i^n$ with the average $(u_i^{n+1}+u_i^{n-1})/2$.

$$
(1+2r)\,u_i^{n+1}=(1-2r)\,u_i^{n-1}+2r\left(u_{i+1}^n+u_{i-1}^n\right)
$$

It is still explicit, so no matrix solve. It needs three time levels, though, and the first step has to come from somewhere else. The original homework took one FTCS step first.

Laasonen is the fully implicit (BTCS) scheme. Every step solves a tridiagonal system.

$$
-r\,u_{i-1}^{n+1}+(1+2r)\,u_i^{n+1}-r\,u_{i+1}^{n+1}=u_i^n
$$

Try it yourself below. On the left are the two roots of the DuFort–Frankel amplification factor. On the right is how one Fourier mode's amplitude evolves step by step.

<DufortRootLocusLab />

Slide $r$ slowly past about 3.2. The two roots meet on the real axis and split up and down. From that moment the green curve crosses zero and goes negative. Both roots always stay inside the dashed circle, which is why nothing blows up.

## Stable, so why wrong? The two roots of the amplification factor

Plug in $u_i^n=G^n e^{\mathrm{i}\,i\theta}$ and you get a quadratic in $G$. Here $\theta=k\Delta x$ is the mode's phase per cell.

$$
G_\pm=\frac{2r\cos\theta\pm\sqrt{1-4r^2\sin^2\theta}}{1+2r}
$$

When the radicand goes negative, the roots are complex conjugates. Their magnitude then no longer depends on $\theta$.

$$
|G_\pm|^2=\frac{2r-1}{2r+1}<1
$$

It is below 1 for every $r$. That is all "unconditionally stable" means. A complex $G$ does more than shrink the amplitude, though. It rotates the phase every step, so the amplitude behaves like $\rho^n\cos(n\varphi+\psi)$. Heat-equation modes never oscillate. They only decay monotonically.

The roots turn complex at $2r\sin\theta=1$. For the fundamental mode, $\theta=\Delta x=\pi/20$, that gives $r^*=1/(2\sin\theta)=3.196$. Shorter wavelengths have larger $\sin\theta$ and start oscillating earlier.

Below the threshold is not safe either. At $r=2$ the fundamental mode has $|G_+|=0.9462$, while the exact decay per step is $e^{-\alpha\Delta t}=0.9518$. It cools 0.6% too fast every step. After 60 steps that adds up to 30%.

## The modified equation: a wave term hiding in the heat equation

The reason sits in the truncation error. A Taylor expansion of DuFort–Frankel gives the equation it actually solves (the modified equation).

$$
u_t=\alpha u_{xx}-\alpha\left(\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta x}\right)^2u_{tt}+\frac{\alpha\Delta x^2}{12}u_{xxxx}+\cdots
$$

The $u_{tt}$ term is the culprit. Its coefficient is $(\Delta t/\Delta x)^2$. Refine $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$ together at a fixed ratio and it never goes away. DuFort–Frankel is consistent with the heat equation only when $\Delta t/\Delta x\to 0$. This is called conditional consistency.

With $\lambda=\Delta t/\Delta x$, the equation becomes the telegraph equation (a damped wave equation).

$$
\alpha\lambda^2u_{tt}+u_t=\alpha u_{xx}
$$

The wave speed is $1/\lambda=\Delta x/\Delta t$, one cell per step, exactly the fastest an explicit scheme can carry information. Insert a mode $e^{\mathrm{i}kx-\sigma t}$ and you get $\alpha\lambda^2\sigma^2-\sigma+\alpha k^2=0$. For a real $\sigma$ you need $4\alpha^2\lambda^2k^2\le 1$, i.e. $\Delta t\le\Delta x/(2\alpha k)$. In terms of $r$ that is $r\le 1/(2k\Delta x)=3.18$, nearly the 3.196 from the amplification factor. The two analyses point to the same boundary.

## Code: the sin x bar at r = 0.5, 2, 5, 10

Same settings as the homework, marched to $t\approx1.5$. All three schemes run at the same $r$, and the script prints the center temperature, the maximum error, and the minimum center temperature seen during the run.

```python
import numpy as np

ALPHA, PEAK, NX = 2.0, 1.0, 21          # same setup as source HW#2
DX = np.pi / (NX - 1)
X = np.linspace(0.0, np.pi, NX)
T_END = 1.5

def exact_u(t):
    return PEAK * np.exp(-ALPHA * t) * np.sin(X)

def ftcs_heat(u, r):
    v = u.copy()
    v[1:-1] = u[1:-1] + r * (u[2:] - 2 * u[1:-1] + u[:-2])
    return v

def laasonen_heat(u, r):
    n = NX - 2                           # tridiagonal on interior nodes (Thomas)
    a = np.full(n, -r); b = np.full(n, 1 + 2 * r); c = np.full(n, -r)
    d = u[1:-1].copy()
    for i in range(1, n):
        m = a[i] / b[i - 1]
        b[i] -= m * c[i - 1]; d[i] -= m * d[i - 1]
    s = np.zeros(n); s[-1] = d[-1] / b[-1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        s[i] = (d[i] - c[i] * s[i + 1]) / b[i]
    v = np.zeros_like(u); v[1:-1] = s
    return v

def dufort_step(u_old, u, r):
    v = np.zeros_like(u)
    v[1:-1] = ((1 - 2 * r) * u_old[1:-1] + 2 * r * (u[2:] + u[:-2])) / (1 + 2 * r)
    return v

def march_heat(method, r, start="ftcs"):
    dt = r * DX**2 / ALPHA
    nstep = max(2, int(round(T_END / dt)))
    u = PEAK * np.sin(X)
    hist = [u[NX // 2]]
    if method == "dufort":
        u_old = u
        u = ftcs_heat(u, r) if start == "ftcs" else exact_u(dt)
        hist.append(u[NX // 2])
        for _ in range(nstep - 1):
            u_old, u = u, dufort_step(u_old, u, r)
            hist.append(u[NX // 2])
    else:
        step = ftcs_heat if method == "ftcs" else laasonen_heat
        for _ in range(nstep):
            u = step(u, r)
            hist.append(u[NX // 2])
    t = nstep * dt
    err = np.max(np.abs(u - exact_u(t)))
    return t, nstep, u[NX // 2], exact_u(t)[NX // 2], err, np.array(hist)

print(f"dx = {DX:.5f}, r* (DF fundamental mode turns complex) = {1/(2*np.sin(DX)):.3f}")
print(" r     method    steps   t      u_mid     exact     max_err   min(u_mid) over run")
for r in [0.5, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0]:
    for m in ["ftcs", "dufort", "laasonen"]:
        t, n, um, ue, e, h = march_heat(m, r)
        if not np.isfinite(e) or e > 1.0:
            print(f"{r:4.1f}  {m:9s} {n:5d}  {t:5.3f}   BLOWUP (|u|max = {np.max(np.abs(h)):.1e})")
            continue
        print(f"{r:4.1f}  {m:9s} {n:5d}  {t:5.3f}  {um:8.5f}  {ue:8.5f}  {e:.2e}  {h.min():+.4f}")
print()
print("DuFort-Frankel per-step |G| of the fundamental mode vs exact decay per step")
for r in [0.5, 2.0, 3.0, 3.2, 5.0, 10.0]:
    dt = r * DX**2 / ALPHA
    th = DX
    disc = 1 - 4 * r * r * np.sin(th)**2
    G = (2 * r * np.cos(th) + np.sqrt(complex(disc))) / (1 + 2 * r)
    print(f"r = {r:4.1f}  |G| = {abs(G):.4f}  exact = {np.exp(-ALPHA*dt):.4f}  "
          f"phase/step = {np.degrees(np.angle(G)):6.2f} deg")
print()
print("DuFort-Frankel start-up: FTCS first step vs exact first step (r = 5)")
for s in ["ftcs", "exact"]:
    t, n, um, ue, e, h = march_heat("dufort", 5.0, start=s)
    print(f"start = {s:5s}  u_mid = {um:+.5f}  max_err = {e:.3e}")
```

## Comparison matrix: what you gain and what you give up

| Item | FTCS | DuFort–Frankel | Laasonen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stability | $r\le 1/2$ | unconditional | unconditional |
| Consistency | unconditional | only if $\Delta t/\Delta x\to0$ | unconditional |
| Time accuracy | $O(\Delta t)$ | $O(\Delta t^2)$ + $O(\lambda^2)$ | $O(\Delta t)$ |
| Cost per step | pointwise update | pointwise, 2 levels stored | tridiagonal solve |
| Start-up | immediate | separate first step | immediate |
| Max error at $r=0.5$ | 6.15e-04 | 6.15e-04 | 1.23e-03 |
| Center temp at $r=10$ | diverges | $-0.827$ | $+0.071$ |
| How it fails | odd-even blow-up | quiet oscillation below zero | decays too slowly |

Three things stand out in the output. First, at $r=0.5$ FTCS and DuFort–Frankel agree to five decimal places. That is also why the homework's mean errors, 0.8954e-3 and 0.8976e-3, were almost identical. Second, at $r=10$ Laasonen's error is 1.95e-02. That is large, but the sign is right. Third, how far FTCS diverges depends on $r$ and the number of steps. At $r=10$ there are only 12 steps, so it stopped at 6.3.

FTCS can't hide its failure. The numbers explode and anyone notices. DuFort–Frankel fails quietly. The values stay finite and the profile is still a $\sin x$. Only the sign is wrong. Even a convergence-order study won't catch it if you refine with $\Delta t/\Delta x$ held fixed, because this error never goes to zero. It is the same trap as in [the verification case that reported two different orders](/en/posts/2026-10-07-sod-vortex-order-verification/).

## Why the two schemes gave identical numbers at r = 0.5

Set $r=1/2$ and the $(1-2r)u_i^{n-1}$ term in DuFort–Frankel vanishes.

$$
u_i^{n+1}=\tfrac12\left(u_{i+1}^n+u_{i-1}^n\right)
$$

FTCS with $r=1/2$ reduces to exactly the same formula. Same formula, same error. The homework attributed DuFort–Frankel's error to accumulation. In fact, at $r=0.5$ there was no difference between the two methods to observe. The difference shows up only for $r>1/2$, which is precisely the regime where you would choose DuFort–Frankel.

I also checked the start-up step. At $r=5$, starting with FTCS gives a center temperature of $-0.0916$, and starting with the exact solution gives $-0.0902$. As the homework observed, the first step hardly matters. But the cause isn't error accumulation. It is the oscillation created by the complex roots.

This isn't unique to diffusion. We saw a single Courant number tip a scheme over in [the Courant limit of the MSTACS compressive scheme](/en/posts/2026-10-25-mstacs-slope-three-courant-limit/). If a large $\Delta t$ is the goal, implicit schemes are the ones that guarantee both stability and accuracy. For unsteady problems it is often better to accept inner iterations, as in [a dual-time loop](/en/posts/2026-10-30-dual-time-steady-preconditioner-inner-iterations/).

## The ratio to compute before handing DuFort–Frankel a Δt

Pick the shortest wavelength $\ell$ you need to resolve, so $k=2\pi/\ell$. Then check one inequality.

$$
\Delta t\le\frac{\Delta x}{2\alpha k}
$$

Cross that line and that wavelength oscillates in time. Stay under it with a large $\Delta t/\Delta x$ and the decay is still too fast. "Unconditionally stable" only means it won't blow up. It doesn't mean it is solving the right equation.
