## A more accurate area gave a worse answer

The gradient of $\phi = x$ is 1. A bent grid does not change that. Feed a curvilinear FVM the cell area integrated along the real curves and the gradient misses by 0.19%. The cell-center Jacobian opens the gap to 0.39%. Four corners, crossed as diagonals, stop at $10^{-14}$.

The starting point is a graduate CFD homework problem. Find the 2D $\Delta V$ from the cross product of two diagonals. Five lines by hand. The homework never asks why those lines beat the curved integral. The reason is one divergence-theorem identity.

## The homework answer: two diagonals give the quad area

Cell $(i,j)$ in computational space maps to a quadrilateral ABCD in physical space. Its corners sit at half-integer indices $(i\pm\tfrac12, j\pm\tfrac12)$. In 2D we take unit depth, so volume equals area.

Treat diagonals $\overline{BD}$ and $\overline{CA}$ as vectors. Translate them to a common origin and they span a parallelogram EFGH. Its area is the cross-product magnitude $|\mathbf{BD}\times\mathbf{CA}|$. That parallelogram is exactly twice the area of the original quad.

$$
\Delta V_{i,j} = \tfrac12\Big[(x_{i+\frac12,j+\frac12}-x_{i-\frac12,j-\frac12})(y_{i-\frac12,j+\frac12}-y_{i+\frac12,j-\frac12}) - (y_{i+\frac12,j+\frac12}-y_{i-\frac12,j-\frac12})(x_{i-\frac12,j+\frac12}-x_{i+\frac12,j-\frac12})\Big]
$$

The first pair is the $x$ component of one diagonal times the $y$ component of the other. The second pair is the opposite combination. Their difference is the 2D cross product (the signed area of the parallelogram two vectors span).

## Why the diagonal cross product is not an approximation

Cut the quad along diagonal AC into triangles ABC and ACD. Each triangle's area is half the cross product of its edge vectors. Adding them groups neatly.

$$
\tfrac12\,\mathbf{AB}\times\mathbf{AC} + \tfrac12\,\mathbf{AC}\times\mathbf{AD} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{AC}\times(\mathbf{AD}-\mathbf{AB}) = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{AC}\times\mathbf{BD}
$$

Since $\mathbf{AD}-\mathbf{AB}=\mathbf{BD}$, the last form follows. The identity holds for convex and concave quads alike. So the diagonal formula equals the area of the polygon whose corners are joined by straight lines. It gives the same value as the shoelace formula (the cross-multiplied corner-coordinate sum for polygon area).

But that is the area of a straight-edged polygon. If the grid lines are truly curved, the real area is $\iint J\,d\xi\,d\eta$. Here $J$ is the Jacobian (the area stretch factor from computational to physical coordinates). The two differ by $O(h^2)$. So is the true area the better choice? The opposite.

## What the Green–Gauss gradient demands of the volume

The Green–Gauss gradient (using the divergence theorem to turn a cell-average gradient into a sum over faces) reads:

$$
(\nabla\phi)_P \approx \frac{1}{V_P}\sum_{f} \phi_f\,\mathbf{S}_f
$$

$\phi_f$ is the face-midpoint value, $\mathbf{S}_f$ is the face normal times face length, and $V_P$ is the cell volume. In an FVM code, faces are straight segments between corners. No face follows the curved grid line.

Plug in $\phi=x$. On a straight edge $x$ is linear, so its midpoint value equals the edge average exactly. The numerator then is the divergence theorem applied to the straight-edged polygon.

$$
\sum_f x_f\,S_{x,f} = \oint_{\partial\Omega_\text{poly}} x\,n_x\,dS = |\Omega_\text{poly}|
$$

$\Omega_\text{poly}$ is the polygon formed by joining corners with straight lines. The gradient therefore equals $|\Omega_\text{poly}|/V_P$. For that to be 1, $V_P$ must be the polygon area, which is the diagonal cross-product area. Feed in the true curved area and the numerator and denominator measure different shapes.

The same mismatch shows up when you discretize the metric terms from [curvilinear coordinate transformation and grid metrics](/en/posts/2026-06-17-curvilinear-coordinate-transformation-grid-metrics/). Face vectors and volumes must come from the same corner data.

## Code: same faces, three volumes

The wavy grid is $x=\xi+A\sin 2\pi\xi\,\sin 2\pi\eta$, $y=\eta+A\sin 2\pi\xi\,\sin 2\pi\eta$ with $A=0.06$. The boundary stays straight while interior grid lines bend. The face fluxes are identical in all three cases; only the volume changes: (1) the diagonal cross product, (2) the analytic $J\cdot h^2$ at the cell center, (3) the true curved area from 4×4 Gauss quadrature.

```python
import math

A = 0.06  # grid waviness amplitude
TP = 2 * math.pi

def mapping(xi, eta):
    w = A * math.sin(TP * xi) * math.sin(TP * eta)
    return xi + w, eta + w

def jac(xi, eta):
    sx, cx = math.sin(TP * xi), math.cos(TP * xi)
    se, ce = math.sin(TP * eta), math.cos(TP * eta)
    wx, we = A * TP * cx * se, A * TP * sx * ce
    return (1 + wx) * (1 + we) - we * wx

def wavy_nodes(n):
    s = [i / n for i in range(n + 1)]
    x = [[mapping(si, sj)[0] for sj in s] for si in s]
    y = [[mapping(si, sj)[1] for sj in s] for si in s]
    return s, x, y

def diag_area(x, y, i, j):
    # 1/2 (BD x CA): cross product of the two diagonals
    d1x = x[i + 1][j + 1] - x[i][j]; d1y = y[i + 1][j + 1] - y[i][j]
    d2x = x[i][j + 1] - x[i + 1][j]; d2y = y[i][j + 1] - y[i + 1][j]
    return 0.5 * (d1x * d2y - d1y * d2x)

def jacobian_area(s, i, j):
    # analytic J at the cell centre times h^2
    h = s[1] - s[0]
    return jac(s[i] + h / 2, s[j] + h / 2) * h * h

GL = [(-0.8611363115940526, 0.3478548451374538), (-0.3399810435848563, 0.6521451548625461),
      (0.3399810435848563, 0.6521451548625461), (0.8611363115940526, 0.3478548451374538)]

def exact_cell_area(s, i, j):
    # area of the curved cell: 4x4 Gauss-Legendre on J
    h = s[1] - s[0]; area = 0.0
    for a, wa in GL:
        for b, wb in GL:
            area += wa * wb * jac(s[i] + h * (1 + a) / 2, s[j] + h * (1 + b) / 2)
    return area * h * h / 4

def green_gauss_dx(x, y, i, j, vol):
    # d(phi)/dx for phi = x; straight faces, counter-clockwise
    p = [(i, j), (i + 1, j), (i + 1, j + 1), (i, j + 1)]
    flux = 0.0
    for k in range(4):
        (ia, ja), (ib, jb) = p[k], p[(k + 1) % 4]
        nx = y[ib][jb] - y[ia][ja]   # outward normal (x) times face length
        flux += 0.5 * (x[ia][ja] + x[ib][jb]) * nx
    return flux / vol

print("  N   err(diag)  err(J centre)  err(exact curved)  order(J)")
prev = None
for n in (8, 16, 32, 64, 128):
    s, x, y = wavy_nodes(n)
    e = [0.0, 0.0, 0.0]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            vols = (diag_area(x, y, i, j), jacobian_area(s, i, j), exact_cell_area(s, i, j))
            for k, v in enumerate(vols):
                e[k] = max(e[k], abs(green_gauss_dx(x, y, i, j, v) - 1.0))
    rate = "   -" if prev is None else f"  {math.log2(prev / e[1]):.2f}"
    print(f"{n:3d}   {e[0]:.1e}    {e[1]:.3e}      {e[2]:.3e}     {rate}")
    prev = e[1]

s, x, y = wavy_nodes(32)
tot_d = sum(diag_area(x, y, i, j) for i in range(32) for j in range(32))
tot_e = sum(exact_cell_area(s, i, j) for i in range(32) for j in range(32))
print(f"total area  diag {tot_d:.15f}  exact {tot_e:.15f}")

def bowtie_area(pts):
    (ax, ay), (bx, by), (cx, cy), (dx, dy) = pts
    return 0.5 * ((cx - ax) * (dy - by) - (cy - ay) * (dx - bx))

for t in (0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0):
    a = bowtie_area([(0, 0), (1, 0), (1 - t, 1), (0, 1)])
    print(f"C at x={1 - t:+.0f}: signed area {a:+.3f}")
```

## Between 1.1e-14 and 3.9e-3

At $N=32$ the diagonal area gives an error of $1.1\times10^{-14}$, round-off level. The cell-center $J$ gives $3.881\times10^{-3}$ and the true curved area gives $1.935\times10^{-3}$. Both drop by a factor of four when the grid is halved. That is order 2.00.

The total area is 1 for all three methods. The global sum is right. The per-cell split is wrong. A global conservation check never catches this.

The 0.19% at $N=32$ is not a second measurement. It is the curved-area error $1.935\times10^{-3}$, read as a percent. On that same row the cell-center $J$ is $3.881\times10^{-3}$, the 0.39% from the opening. The diagonal entry is $1.1\times10^{-14}$. Same faces, same numerator. Only the denominator $V_P$ changes.

Down the $J$ column, $N=8$ is $6.032\times10^{-2}$. Then $1.543\times10^{-2}$, $3.881\times10^{-3}$, $9.716\times10^{-4}$, $2.430\times10^{-4}$ at $N=16,32,64,128$. The printed orders are 1.97, 1.99, 2.00, 2.00. From $N=8$ to $N=128$ the grid is 16 times finer and the error falls by about 248. Second order says $4^{4}=256$. The gap between 248 and 256 lives in that first 1.97. After $N=8$, each step is almost exactly a factor of four.

The curved-area column drops alongside: $2.896\times10^{-2}$, $7.638\times10^{-3}$, $1.935\times10^{-3}$, $4.855\times10^{-4}$, $1.215\times10^{-4}$. Every row sits near half the center-$J$ error. At $N=8$ the ratio $2.896/6.032$ is about 0.48. At $N=128$ it is $1.215/2.430=0.50$. A 4×4 Gauss rule beats one sample at the center. The numerator still measures the straight polygon $|\Omega_\text{poly}|$, so the gradient $|\Omega_\text{poly}|/V_P$ leaves 1. Both totals print as 1.000000000000000. Cell-wise excess and deficit cancel. One cell's $\partial x/\partial x$ never sees that cancellation.

An $O(h^2)$ error is easy to wave off inside a second-order scheme. A linear field that is not reproduced exactly shakes the premise of second-order reconstruction. Limiters also react to this fake gradient. It stacks on the skewness errors in [Green–Gauss versus least-squares gradients](/en/posts/2026-07-17-least-squares-gradient-reconstruction-unstructured/).

## The signal this formula gives on a twisted cell

The last four lines of the output come from dragging corner C to the left. With C at $x=+1$ the area is 1.000, and at $x=0$ it is 0.500. At $x=-1$ it is 0.000, and at $x=-2$ it is $-0.500$.

Once C passes D, edges BC and DA cross. The cell twists into a bow-tie. The diagonal formula then returns the signed sum of the two triangle areas. One triangle goes negative, so the sum falls to zero or below.

That is a signal, not a defect. The sign of $\Delta V$, checked on every cell right after grid generation, marks the twisted ones. An absolute value kills the signal. A twisted cell then enters the solver as a normal cell with positive volume.

## If ∂x/∂x is not 1, do not use that volume

The analytic $J$ is not trash. It is still a grid-quality metric and an error indicator. The $V_P$ in the FVM update denominator has to come from the same corners as the face vectors $\mathbf{S}_f$. Drop in the curved integral $\iint J\,d\xi\,d\eta$ and the denominator is looking at a different shape.

The check is short. Put $\phi=x$ and $\phi=y$ on a bent grid and print the Green–Gauss gradient. If $\partial x/\partial x$ is not 1 at round-off, that volume stays out of the update. The five diagonal lines from the homework are the short way through the check.
