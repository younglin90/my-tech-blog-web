## The Same Bar Only Blew Up When I Pulled On It

I ran the same material through the same code twice. Once squeezed, once stretched. The squeezed run
finished. The stretched run had particles sticking together in twos, with holes opening between them,
and then it died. This post measures where that asymmetry comes from using a single eigenvalue, and
then sorts out what each of the published cures actually charges you. By the end you will know what to
look at first — instead of the stress magnitude — when an SPH tension run falls over.

The source is the SPH review Xu and coauthors published in *Acta Mechanica Sinica* (2023). It sweeps
fluid, solid and biomechanics, and the solid section sets tensile instability aside as a standing
challenge, listing stress points, conservative smoothing, artificial stress and Total Lagrangian SPH
side by side. Four cures for one problem seemed worth measuring directly.

## The Feedback Loop That Opens When the Kernel Recounts Its Neighbours

The SPH momentum equation looks like this, with stress $\sigma$ positive in tension.

$$
\frac{dv_i}{dt}=\sum_j m_j\left(\frac{\sigma_i}{\rho_i^{2}}+\frac{\sigma_j}{\rho_j^{2}}\right)
\frac{\partial W_{ij}}{\partial x_i}
$$

Here $m_j$ is particle mass, $\rho_i$ is density, and $W_{ij}=W(|x_i-x_j|/h)$ is the kernel built on
smoothing length $h$. Density does not come from a grid — it comes from counting neighbours.

$$
\rho_i=\sum_j m_j\,W(|x_i-x_j|/h)
$$

A trap hides in there. When particles move, both $W_{ij}$ and $\partial W_{ij}/\partial x_i$ move
with them. Deformation generates force, and that force then edits the very tool used to compute
force. Finite elements have no such loop, because shape functions are pinned to the node layout. SPH
has one, because it rebuilds the kernel from current positions every step.

## One Sign Decides It

What Swegle settled in 1995 is a statement about a sign combination, not about a magnitude. Put a
small displacement of wavenumber $k$ on a uniformly stressed particle layout, linearize, and the
frequency comes out as a difference of two terms.

$$
\rho_0\,\omega^{2}(k)=E\,A(k)-\sigma_0\,B(k)
$$

$A(k)$ comes from elastic stiffness and is never negative. $B(k)$ comes from rebuilding the kernel,
and it is a sum that collects $W''(r_{ij})$ over neighbours. Once $\omega^2$ turns negative, $\omega$
becomes purely imaginary and the displacement grows exponentially. Reduce the condition to a single
neighbour and you get the famous form.

$$
\sigma\,W''(r_{ij})\gt 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{unstable}
$$

That explains the asymmetry. The second derivative of the cubic B-spline kernel flips sign once
$r/h$ passes roughly $2/3$. In tension ($\sigma\gt 0$) the distant neighbours are the dangerous ones;
in compression ($\sigma\lt 0$) the close ones are. On a real layout most neighbours sit on the
$r/h\gt 2/3$ side, so the danger piles up on the tension side. The place where $W''$ of the cubic
B-spline changes sign near $r/h \approx 2/3$ is the problem that comes after picking the kernel in
[SPH, from the kernel through artificial viscosity](/en/posts/2026-04-29-sph-meshfree-particle-hydrodynamics/).

## Measuring the Growth Rate as an Eigenvalue Directly

The sign condition is per neighbour. What you need from real code is the growth rate of the fastest
mode on the whole layout. Time integration mixes in lattice noise and time error, so it is cleaner to
extract the stiffness matrix of the acceleration operator by numerical differentiation and look at
its eigenvalues. On a periodic one-dimensional particle row with $E=\rho_0=1$ and $\Delta x=1$, the
rate is directly in units of $c/\Delta x$.

```python
import numpy as np

def spline_w(q):
    w = np.zeros_like(q)
    a, b = q < 1.0, (q >= 1.0) & (q < 2.0)
    w[a] = 1.0 - 1.5 * q[a] ** 2 + 0.75 * q[a] ** 3
    w[b] = 0.25 * (2.0 - q[b]) ** 3
    return (2.0 / 3.0) * w

def spline_dw(q):
    d = np.zeros_like(q)
    a, b = q < 1.0, (q >= 1.0) & (q < 2.0)
    d[a] = -3.0 * q[a] + 2.25 * q[a] ** 2
    d[b] = -0.75 * (2.0 - q[b]) ** 2
    return (2.0 / 3.0) * d

def pair_tables(x, h, L):
    d = x[:, None] - x[None, :]
    d -= L * np.round(d / L)                 # shortest separation under periodic boundaries
    q = np.abs(d) / h
    dW = spline_dw(q) / h ** 2 * np.sign(d)  # dW/dx_i
    np.fill_diagonal(dW, 0.0)
    return spline_w(q) / h, dW

def eulerian_accel(u, x0, h, L, m, rho0, E, sig0):
    """classic SPH: the kernel is rebuilt from the deformed current positions."""
    W, dW = pair_tables(x0 + u, h, L)
    rho = m * W.sum(axis=1)
    sig = sig0 + E * (rho0 / rho - 1.0)
    return (m * ((sig / rho ** 2)[:, None] + (sig / rho ** 2)[None, :]) * dW).sum(axis=1)

def lagrangian_accel(u, x0, h, L, m, rho0, E, sig0):
    """total-Lagrangian SPH: the kernel is frozen on the initial lattice."""
    _, dW0 = pair_tables(x0, h, L)
    F = 1.0 + ((m / rho0) * (u[None, :] - u[:, None]) * dW0).sum(axis=1)
    P = sig0 + E * (F - 1.0)
    return (m / rho0 ** 2 * (P[:, None] + P[None, :]) * dW0).sum(axis=1)

def worst_mode(accel, x0, h, L, m, rho0, E, sig0, eps=1e-7):
    """pull the stiffness matrix out by numerical differentiation, then find the fastest growing displacement mode."""
    n = len(x0)
    K = np.zeros((n, n))
    for j in range(n):
        p = np.zeros(n)
        p[j] = eps
        K[:, j] = (accel(p, x0, h, L, m, rho0, E, sig0)
                   - accel(-p, x0, h, L, m, rho0, E, sig0)) / (2.0 * eps)
    lam, V = np.linalg.eig(K)                # u'' = K u, so omega^2 = -lam
    i = int(np.argmax(lam.real))
    rate = np.sqrt(max(lam[i].real, 0.0))    # growth rate in units of c/dx
    spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(V[:, i].real))
    kmode = int(np.argmax(spec[1:])) + 1
    return rate, L / kmode

n, dx, rho0, E = 64, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0
x0 = np.arange(n) * dx
L, m = n * dx, rho0 * dx

print("sigma0/E   h/dx   Eulerian rate   worst lambda/dx   frozen-kernel rate")
for sig in (+0.05, 0.0, -0.05):
    for hf in (1.0, 1.2, 1.5):
        ge, lam = worst_mode(eulerian_accel, x0, hf * dx, L, m, rho0, E, sig * E)
        gl, _ = worst_mode(lagrangian_accel, x0, hf * dx, L, m, rho0, E, sig * E)
        tag = f"{lam:.2f}" if ge > 1e-4 else "none"       # no growing mode means no wavelength
        print(f"{sig:+8.2f}   {hf:4.2f}   {ge:13.6f}   {tag:>15}   {gl:18.6f}")

print("\nwidening the kernel at sigma0/E = +0.05")
print(" h/dx   rate   worst lambda/dx")
for hf in (1.0, 1.2, 1.4, 1.5, 1.7, 2.0):
    ge, lam = worst_mode(eulerian_accel, x0, hf * dx, L, m, rho0, E, +0.05 * E)
    print(f"{hf:5.2f}   {ge:6.4f}   {lam:13.2f}")

print("\nrate scaling with the pre-stress at h/dx = 1.2")
for sig in (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20):
    ge, _ = worst_mode(eulerian_accel, x0, 1.2 * dx, L, m, rho0, E, sig * E)
    print(f"sigma0/E = {sig:+5.2f}   rate = {ge:6.4f}   rate/sqrt(sigma0/E) = {ge / np.sqrt(sig):6.4f}")
```

## The Fastest Growing Mode Had Wavelength $2\Delta x$

Only the three tension rows of the first table carry a nonzero rate. At $h/\Delta x=1.2$ it is
0.3334; in compression it is zero. The values at the $10^{-5}$ level are round-off on the rigid
translation mode, so read them as zero.

The interesting column is the next one. The fastest growing mode has wavelength $2.00\,\Delta x$ —
the shortest wavelength the layout can represent, with every particle pushed alternately left and
right. That is why the symptom on screen is a particle pair. Two particles close in on each other,
and next to them sits a hole with nobody in it. The density summation reads $\rho$ low in that hole,
which makes the stress there even more tensile, and the loop closes. One particle spacing steers the
whole field.

The third table says the rate rides on $\sqrt{\sigma_0}$. The ratio in the last column stays between
1.38 and 1.51. Quadruple the tension and the rate doubles. It also means a mere 1% stress still gives
a rate of 0.1375. Run long enough and it blows up anyway.

0.6325 / 0.0488 = 13.0, which is the factor of 13 from $h/\Delta x = 1.0$ to 1.5. The rate 0.1335
at 2.0 is 2.7 times 0.0488 at 1.5. The worst wavelength stretches from 2.00 to 3.20. Dividing
0.6761 at $\sigma_0/E = 0.20$ by 0.3334 at 0.05 gives 2.03. Since $\sqrt{0.20/0.05} = 2$, that is
the statement that four times the stress doubles the rate. The last-column entries 1.4912 and
1.5119 sit next to each other. $0.1375 / \sqrt{0.01} = 1.375$, matching the table's 1.3755. At
tension +0.05 and $h/\Delta x = 1.2$, the Eulerian rate 0.333437 sits beside a frozen-kernel value
of 0.000012, more than twenty thousand times smaller. At $h/\Delta x = 1.0$ the pair is 0.632456
against 0.000000, so the frozen column is zero. The $4\times10^{-6}$ at compression $-0.05$ and
$h/\Delta x = 1.2$ is round-off on the rigid mode, not growth.

## Does Widening $h$ Make It Go Away?

The second table is the most counterintuitive part of this post. Raising $h/\Delta x$ from 1.0 to 1.5
cuts the rate from 0.6325 to 0.0488, a factor of 13. So far, as expected. But past 1.5 it climbs back
up: 0.0920 at 1.7, and 0.1335 at 2.0.

The wavelength column explains it. The worst mode, $2.00\,\Delta x$ up to $h/\Delta x = 1.4$, becomes
$2.29\,\Delta x$ at 1.5 and $3.20\,\Delta x$ at 2.0. Widening $h$ pulls more neighbours inside
$r=2h$. If a newly admitted neighbour's $W''$ has the destabilizing sign again, the instability does
not disappear — it relocates to a longer wavelength.

Why that is awkward in practice is clear enough. Longer-wavelength instabilities are harder to spot.
Particles pairing up is obvious from the log alone. A three-cell clump looks like "physical
localization". Widening $h$ is less a treatment of the symptom than a way of making it harder to
recognize.

## Tying the Kernel to the Initial Layout

The last column of the table is the cure. Total Lagrangian SPH computes the kernel and its derivative
once on the initial configuration $X$ and holds them fixed forever. Deformation enters through the
deformation gradient.

$$
F_i=1+\sum_j \frac{m_j}{\rho_0}\,(u_j-u_i)\,\frac{\partial W^{0}_{ij}}{\partial X_i},
\qquad P_i=\sigma_0+E\,(F_i-1)
$$

Here $u$ is displacement, $W^0$ is the kernel built on the initial configuration, and $P$ is the
first Piola–Kirchhoff stress. Position no longer appears inside $W^0$, so the path by which
deformation edits the kernel is cut. The rate coming out as zero at every stress level is the result. Stability is decided by which
identities the discrete operators inherit intact.

The bill is equally clear. Because the initial neighbour list is used to the end, problems where the
neighbour relations themselves change — fracture, fragmentation, very large deformation — are out of
reach. New contacts cannot form either. So solid impact codes keep the TL formulation, bolt a
separate failure model onto it, and pick the moments to refresh neighbours by hand.

## Where Stress Points, Artificial Stress and $\delta$-SPH Each Live

The remaining prescriptions in the review each touch a different path.

Stress points put displacement and stress at different locations. The $2\Delta x$ mode then produces
a nonzero strain at the stress points, so the worst mode from earlier acquires stiffness. It is the
same principle by which a staggered layout kills checkerboard modes.

Artificial stress adds a repulsive term in tensile regions that only acts at short range. It does not
fix $\sigma W''\gt 0$ directly; it covers it with a force in the opposite direction. The coefficient
choice changes the answer, so the verification burden is heavy.

Conservative smoothing filters the strain field every step. Short wavelengths get shaved, so the
worst mode dies first — but genuine physical stress localization gets shaved along with it.

$\delta$-SPH is not really a tensile-instability prescription at all. It adds a diffusion term to the
density field to reduce pressure noise in weakly compressible fluid runs. The review puts the two on
the same page because the symptoms look alike, but the causes differ. Fluid-side noise arises because
the equation of state amplifies density error. Solid-side pairing comes from the product of the
stress sign and $W''$. Borrow one prescription for the other problem and you only mask the symptom.

## What You Actually Sign For When You Pick a Particle Method

The price SPH paid for erasing the grid reduces to one line: the kernel depends on the solution. In
compression-dominated problems that dependence looks free. The invoice arrives the moment tension
enters.

The question to ask about a code is not how many stabilization options it ships. It is only two
things: how long tension is sustained in this calculation, and whether neighbour relations have to
change. Long tension with fixed neighbours means the TL formulation is right. Neighbours that must
keep changing means an updating kernel, with the stabilization coefficients promoted to something you
verify. A rate of 0.3334 does not ask how many options you switched on. Which identities a discrete
operator inherits is the same question as
