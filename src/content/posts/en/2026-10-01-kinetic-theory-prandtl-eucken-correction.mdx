## Derive μ and k the same way and Pr has to be γ

Compress air to ten atmospheres and its thermal conductivity barely moves. The density went up
tenfold, yet heat flows at the same rate. Kinetic theory explains that in two lines. But if you
pull the viscosity out of those same two lines, the Prandtl number — the ratio of momentum
diffusivity to thermal diffusivity — gets nailed to $\gamma$. For air that means 1.400. The
measured value is 0.707. This post covers what a number that is 1.98× too large breaks in a CFD
calculation, and exactly what the factor of $9/4$ that Eucken supplied in 1913 repairs.

## What a molecule carries across the tally plane

Plant an imaginary plane inside the flow. The number of molecules crossing it per unit area and
time is

$$
\dot{N}'' = \tfrac{1}{4} n \bar{c}
$$

with $n$ the number density and $\bar{c}$ the mean molecular speed. A molecule crossing downward
had its last collision one mean free path — the average distance a molecule flies between
collisions — above the plane, so it arrives carrying the value from up there. One crossing upward
carries the value from $\lambda$ below. Carry momentum and you get viscosity; carry kinetic
energy and you get conduction.

$$
\mu = \tfrac{1}{3}\rho\,\bar{c}\,\lambda, \qquad k = \tfrac{1}{3}\rho\,\bar{c}\,\lambda\,c_v
$$

Here $\rho = nm$ and $c_v$ is the specific heat at constant volume per unit mass. The two
expressions share every leading factor. Only the trailing $c_v$ differs.

## Why an eightfold density increase leaves conductivity alone

The mean free path is inversely proportional to density. For a collision cross section $\sigma$,

$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\, n \sigma}
$$

so inside $\rho \bar{c} \lambda$ the factors $n$ and $1/n$ erase each other.

$$
k = \frac{m c_v \bar{c}}{3\sqrt{2}\,\sigma}
$$

Density is gone. More molecules means more crossings, but each one starts closer to the plane
and therefore brings a smaller temperature difference with it. Try it in the simulation below.

## Three routes in one table

Now divide the two expressions. The Prandtl number is defined as $\Pr = \mu c_p / k$.

$$
\Pr = \frac{\mu c_p}{k} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{3}\rho\bar{c}\lambda\, c_p}{\tfrac{1}{3}\rho\bar{c}\lambda\, c_v} = \frac{c_p}{c_v} = \gamma
$$

Every instance of $\rho$, $\bar{c}$ and $\lambda$ cancels. Molecular diameter, temperature and
pressure all drop out of the answer. Only the specific heat ratio survives. This is not an
accident of the derivation — it is its structure, because momentum and energy were loaded onto
the same carrier in the same way. It has the same flavor as
[the gap between the continuum viscosity and the lattice viscosity in LBM](/en/posts/2026-09-28-lbm-half-step-viscosity-taylor-expansion/):
no single coefficient is wrong, but two distinct physical processes were tied to one scale.

| Route | Pr formula | Value for air |
|---|---|---|
| One-term mean free path | $\gamma$ | 1.400 |
| Eucken | $4\gamma/(9\gamma-5)$ | 0.737 |
| Measured | — | 0.707 |

## Rotational degrees of freedom carry no momentum

$\Pr = \gamma$ fails because $c_v$ was treated as a single lump. For a diatomic molecule, $c_v$
splits into three translational and two rotational degrees of freedom, and only translation feeds
momentum transport. A spinning dumbbell may cross the plane, but its rotational energy
contributes nothing to the shear stress.

Eucken separated the two parts and gave each its own transport efficiency. Translational energy
is correlated with velocity, so faster molecules carry more of it and it picks up a weight of
$5/2$. Internal energy is uncorrelated with velocity, so its weight is 1. The result is one line.

$$
\frac{k}{\mu c_v} = 1 + \frac{9}{4}(\gamma - 1)
$$

For a monatomic gas with $\gamma \to 5/3$ this becomes $1 + 9/4 \cdot 2/3 = 2.5$, exactly $5/2$:
with no rotation, only the translational weight remains. Rearranged,

$$
\Pr = \frac{\gamma}{1 + \tfrac{9}{4}(\gamma-1)} = \frac{4\gamma}{9\gamma - 5}
$$

## Running all three routes on six gases

The script computes the three routes side by side and then carries the wrong Pr through to a real
calculation: a one-dimensional Couette layer with shear heating. One wall moves at $U$; the
opposite wall is adiabatic.

$$
\frac{d}{dy}\!\left(k\frac{dT}{dy}\right) + \mu\!\left(\frac{du}{dy}\right)^{2} = 0
$$

With $u$ linear the source term is constant, and the temperature rise at the adiabatic wall is
proportional to $\Pr$ alone.

$$
T(0) - T_w = \Pr\,\frac{U^{2}}{2 c_p}
$$

```python
import math

GASES = [
    ("He",  1.667, 0.68),
    ("Ar",  1.667, 0.67),
    ("N2",  1.404, 0.72),
    ("Air", 1.400, 0.707),
    ("CO2", 1.289, 0.77),
    ("H2O", 1.329, 0.98),
]

def pr_mean_free_path(gamma):
    """mu = (1/3) rho c_bar lam,  k = (1/3) rho c_bar lam c_v  ->  Pr = c_p/c_v."""
    return gamma

def pr_eucken(gamma):
    """k/(mu c_v) = 1 + (9/4)(gamma-1)  ->  Pr = 4 gamma / (9 gamma - 5)."""
    return 4.0 * gamma / (9.0 * gamma - 5.0)

print("gas   gamma   Pr_mfp  Pr_euck  Pr_meas   mfp/meas  euck/meas")
for name, g, meas in GASES:
    a, b = pr_mean_free_path(g), pr_eucken(g)
    print("%-5s %5.3f  %6.3f  %7.3f  %7.3f   %8.2f  %9.2f" % (name, g, a, b, meas, a / meas, b / meas))

def couette_wall_rise(Pr, U, cp, N=201):
    """d2T/dy2 = -Pr U^2/cp on y in [0,1]; adiabatic at y=0, T=0 at y=1. Thomas sweep."""
    h = 1.0 / (N - 1)
    src = Pr * U * U / cp
    lo = [0.0] * N; di = [0.0] * N; up = [0.0] * N; rhs = [0.0] * N
    di[0], up[0], rhs[0] = -1.0, 1.0, 0.0
    for i in range(1, N - 1):
        lo[i], di[i], up[i] = 1.0, -2.0, 1.0
        rhs[i] = -src * h * h
    lo[N - 1], di[N - 1], rhs[N - 1] = 0.0, 1.0, 0.0
    for i in range(1, N):
        w = lo[i] / di[i - 1]
        di[i] -= w * up[i - 1]
        rhs[i] -= w * rhs[i - 1]
    T = [0.0] * N
    T[N - 1] = rhs[N - 1] / di[N - 1]
    for i in range(N - 2, -1, -1):
        T[i] = (rhs[i] - up[i] * T[i + 1]) / di[i]
    return T

U, cp = 600.0, 1005.0
print("\nCouette shear layer, U=%.0f m/s, cp=%.0f J/kg/K, adiabatic wall at y=0" % (U, cp))
print("route            Pr    dT_wall[K]   Pr*U^2/(2cp)[K]")
rise = {}
for label, Pr in (("mean-free-path", pr_mean_free_path(1.4)),
                  ("Eucken", pr_eucken(1.4)),
                  ("measured", 0.707)):
    T = couette_wall_rise(Pr, U, cp)
    rise[label] = T[0]
    print("%-15s %5.3f  %10.1f   %14.1f" % (label, Pr, T[0], Pr * U * U / (2 * cp)))

print("\nwall-temperature gap = %.1f K   ratio = %.2f"
      % (rise["mean-free-path"] - rise["measured"], rise["mean-free-path"] / rise["measured"]))

print("\nk ~ sqrt(T) extrapolated from 300 K vs measured air conductivity")
k300 = 0.0263
for T_, k_meas in ((400, 0.0338), (600, 0.0469), (800, 0.0573), (1000, 0.0672)):
    k_law = k300 * math.sqrt(T_ / 300.0)
    print("T=%5d K   sqrtT=%.4f   measured=%.4f   error=%+6.1f%%"
          % (T_, k_law, k_meas, 100.0 * (k_law - k_meas) / k_meas))
```

## A factor of 9/4 decided 123.5 K of wall temperature

The numerical and analytical columns agree to within 1 K — the $O(h^2)$ error on a 201-point
grid. The third column is the one that matters. At $U = 600$ m/s the adiabatic wall sits at
249.5 K above the moving wall if Pr is taken as $\gamma$, and 126.0 K above it with the measured
value. That is a 123.5 K spread. In a supersonic wall heat flux prediction, that gap changes the
material you select.

The Eucken column lands within 2–4% of measurement for every monatomic and diatomic gas listed.

## Why the √T law falls 28.5% short at 1000 K

The same derivation also hands you a temperature dependence. If $\bar{c} \propto \sqrt{T}$ and
$\sigma$ is constant, then

$$
k \propto \sqrt{T}
$$

The last output block extrapolates that law from 300 K. It reads 10.2% low at 400 K and 28.5%
low at 1000 K. The fact that the sign never flips is the clue: the constant-$\sigma$ assumption
is what broke. Faster molecules graze past one another more closely, so the effective cross
section shrinks. This is the slot Chapman–Enskog theory fills with the $\Omega^{(2,2)*}$
collision integral, and that practical codes hand to the constant $S$ in Sutherland's law. The
bookkeeping drift when
[each species gets its own relaxation time](/en/posts/2026-09-23-multicomponent-lbm-correction-velocity/)
in a combustion solver is, in the end, the same question of how far to trust these transport
coefficients.

## Where Eucken misses H₂O by 22%

H₂O is the one row with `euck/meas = 0.78`, 22% low. Water is a polar molecule and dipole
interactions dominate its collisions. Rotational relaxation is far slower than translational, so
the assumption that internal energy rides along uncorrelated with velocity does not hold. The
modified Eucken relation multiplies the internal contribution by its own
$\rho D_{\text{int}}/\mu$ to capture this. In a combustion calculation rich in steam, holding Pr
constant loads that discrepancy straight into the wall heat flux.

## What you actually sign when you type Pr = 0.71

The line `Pr = 0.71` is not a constant declaration. It asserts that momentum and energy diffuse
at different rates even though the same molecules carry both, and that the ratio comes from two
rotational degrees of freedom. Three conditions break that assertion: a monatomic gas (it falls
to 0.67), a polar polyatomic one (it rises to 0.98), and a rarefied gas where $\lambda$ outgrows
the cell (`n = 1` in the first simulation). If your calculation lives in one of those three,
changing a single constant is not the end of the job.
