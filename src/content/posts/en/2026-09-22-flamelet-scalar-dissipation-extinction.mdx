## Diffusion went up, so why did the flame go out

Building a flamelet library forces one arbitrary choice: how far to stretch the scalar dissipation grid $\chi_{st}$ before cutting the table off. Cut too early and the turbulent solver queries outside the table. Cut too late and Newton spins looking for solutions that do not exist. To see that boundary I solved the one-dimensional flamelet equations directly.

The result first. Pushing $\chi_{st}$ from 0.05 to 10.12 1/s — a factor of 202 — dropped the peak temperature from 2258.8 K to 1960.9 K, just 13.2%. One more step and the solution was gone. The temperature does not drift toward zero; the branch that was holding disappears whole.

That sounds backwards. Diffusion is the only thing carrying reactants to the flame surface. In a non-premixed flame the fuel and the oxidizer arrive from opposite sides and have no other way to meet. Raise that diffusion and the fire dies.

Raise the `strain rate a` slider and the mixing layer thins, so $\chi$ at the same $Z$ grows.

## What does it take to write a flame with a single scalar

Assume every species diffuses at the same rate. Under that assumption the mixing state of a fluid particle is fixed by one conserved scalar: the mixture fraction $Z$. Here $Z=0$ is pure oxidizer and $Z=1$ is pure fuel.

Peters proposed in 1984 a coordinate transform that makes $Z$ the independent variable. Written on $Z$ instead of three spatial coordinates, only the direction normal to the flame survives.

$$
\rho \frac{\partial Y_k}{\partial t} = \rho \frac{\chi}{2} \frac{\partial^2 Y_k}{\partial Z^2} + \dot{\omega}_k
$$

$Y_k$ is the mass fraction of species $k$, $\dot{\omega}_k$ its production rate, and $\chi$ the scalar dissipation rate. The energy equation takes the same shape.

$$
\rho \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \rho \frac{\chi}{2} \frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial Z^2} - \frac{1}{c_p}\sum_k h_k \dot{\omega}_k
$$

$T$ is temperature, $c_p$ the heat capacity at constant pressure, $h_k$ the species enthalpies. Ferreira later relaxed the constant-$c_p$ assumption and proposed a more general form, which collapses back to this one when $c_p$ is constant.

What left is the flow. No velocity field, no pressure, no three-dimensional grid. Everything turbulence does to the flame has been compressed into $\chi$ alone. The place where one $\chi$ carries two temperatures at once is [where the flamelet table folds, 1759 K beside 818 K](/en/posts/2026-10-06-flamelet-library-slf-fpv-progress-variable/).

## The inverse of what, exactly, is the scalar dissipation rate

The definition is plain.

$$
\chi = 2D\,\left|\nabla Z\right|^2
$$

$D$ is the diffusivity. The dimension is 1/s: read it as the inverse of the characteristic diffusion time.

When turbulence stretches a flamelet, the gradient of $Z$ steepens. Since $\chi$ rides on the square of that gradient, it grows faster still. More molecular diffusion reaches the reaction surface — and **the heat leaving that surface grows in exactly the same proportion**. Meanwhile the residence time available for reaction shrinks.

That is the answer to the paradox. Diffusion brings the reactants, but it also carries the heat away. Chemistry depends exponentially on temperature, so the side that loses heat always wins first.

For a laminar counterflow flame the $Z$-dependence of $\chi$ has a closed form, the one Pitsch verified against DNS.

$$
\chi(Z) = \chi_{st}\,\frac{\exp\!\left(-2\left[\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(2Z)\right]^2\right)}{\exp\!\left(-2\left[\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(2Z_{st})\right]^2\right)}
$$

$\chi_{st}$ is the value at the stoichiometric mixture fraction $Z_{st}$ — the single number that indexes the library. And $Z_{st}$ follows from the feed compositions alone.

$$Z_{st} = \frac{1}{1 + s\,Y_{F,1}/Y_{O,0}}$$

$s$ is the mass of oxygen needed to burn 1 kg of fuel, $Y_{F,1}$ the fuel mass fraction in the fuel stream, $Y_{O,0}$ the oxygen mass fraction in the oxidizer stream.

## How do you pin the quench limit in code

With a one-step irreversible reaction $F + s\,O \to (1+s)P$ the unknown collapses to a single function. Because the diffusivities are equal, $Y_F + T/q$ is linear in $Z$.

$$
Y_F(Z) + \frac{T(Z)}{q} = \frac{T_0}{q} + Z\left(Y_{F,1} + \frac{T_1 - T_0}{q}\right)
$$

$q$ is the heat release per unit mass of fuel written in temperature units. $Y_O$ ties to $T$ the same way. So there is one field to solve for, $T(Z)$, and one tridiagonal matrix.

The strategy is continuation. Start at a very small $\chi_{st}$ from the Burke–Schumann solution. Raise $\chi_{st}$ by a factor of 1.35 each step, run Newton, and hand the previous solution forward as the initial guess. The moment the burning solution ceases to exist, Newton either fails to converge or falls to room temperature. Forty geometric bisections between the last two values pin the limit.

```python
import math

N = 201
H = 1.0 / (N - 1)
ZG = [i * H for i in range(N)]
T_OX, T_FU = 300.0, 300.0      # inlet temperature on both sides [K]
S_RATIO = 4.0                  # kg of oxygen per kg of fuel
Y_OX = 0.232                   # oxygen mass fraction in air
Q_HEAT = 36000.0               # heat release written as a temperature [K]
T_ACT = 24000.0                # activation temperature [K]
A_PRE = 2.0e10                 # pre-exponential factor [1/s]

def inv_erfc(y):
    lo, hi = -6.0, 6.0
    for _ in range(80):
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if math.erfc(mid) > y: lo = mid
        else: hi = mid
    return 0.5 * (lo + hi)

def chi_profile(chi_st, z_st):
    # Pitsch's counterflow shape. It vanishes at both ends, so put a floor under it
    f = lambda z: math.exp(-2.0 * inv_erfc(2.0 * z) ** 2)
    fst = f(z_st)
    out = []
    for z in ZG:
        v = f(min(max(z, 1e-9), 1 - 1e-9)) / fst
        out.append(chi_st * max(v, 1e-4))
    return out

def couple_species(z, temp, y_fu):
    # coupling functions: recover Y_F and Y_O from T and Z alone
    yf = (T_OX / Q_HEAT + z * (y_fu + T_FU / Q_HEAT - T_OX / Q_HEAT)) - temp / Q_HEAT
    yo = (Y_OX + S_RATIO * T_OX / Q_HEAT
          + z * (S_RATIO * T_FU / Q_HEAT - Y_OX - S_RATIO * T_OX / Q_HEAT)) - S_RATIO * temp / Q_HEAT
    return max(yf, 0.0), max(yo, 0.0)

def tridiag_sweep(a, b, c, d):
    n = len(b)
    cp = [0.0] * n; dp = [0.0] * n
    cp[0] = c[0] / b[0]; dp[0] = d[0] / b[0]
    for i in range(1, n):
        m = b[i] - a[i] * cp[i - 1]
        cp[i] = c[i] / m
        dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i] * dp[i - 1]) / m
    x = [0.0] * n
    x[-1] = dp[-1]
    for i in range(n - 2, -1, -1):
        x[i] = dp[i] - cp[i] * x[i + 1]
    return x

def newton_flamelet(chi, guess, y_fu, itmax=60):
    T = list(guess)
    n = N - 2
    for _ in range(itmax):
        a = [0.0] * n; b = [0.0] * n; c = [0.0] * n; r = [0.0] * n
        for k in range(n):
            i = k + 1
            yf, yo = couple_species(ZG[i], T[i], y_fu)
            ex = math.exp(-T_ACT / T[i])
            w = A_PRE * yf * yo * ex
            dw = A_PRE * ex * (-yo / Q_HEAT - yf * S_RATIO / Q_HEAT + yf * yo * T_ACT / T[i] ** 2)
            if yf <= 0.0 or yo <= 0.0: w = 0.0; dw = 0.0
            cf = chi[i] / (2.0 * H * H)
            r[k] = -(cf * (T[i + 1] - 2 * T[i] + T[i - 1]) + Q_HEAT * w)
            a[k] = cf if k > 0 else 0.0
            b[k] = -2.0 * cf + Q_HEAT * dw
            c[k] = cf if k < n - 1 else 0.0
        d = tridiag_sweep(a, b, c, r)
        lim = max(abs(v) for v in d)
        scale = min(1.0, 200.0 / lim) if lim > 200.0 else 1.0   # limit the temperature update
        for k in range(n):
            T[k + 1] = max(T[k + 1] + scale * d[k], 280.0)
        if lim < 1e-6:
            return T, True
    return T, False

def burke_schumann(y_fu):
    # infinite-rate limit - the starting point of the continuation
    z_st = 1.0 / (1.0 + S_RATIO * y_fu / Y_OX)
    T = []
    for z in ZG:
        if z <= z_st: T.append(T_OX + (T_FU - T_OX) * z + Q_HEAT * y_fu * z)
        else: T.append(T_OX + (T_FU - T_OX) * z + Q_HEAT * y_fu * z_st * (1 - z) / (1 - z_st))
    return z_st, T

def extinction_scan(y_fu, chi0=0.05, growth=1.35, steps=60):
    z_st, T = burke_schumann(y_fu)
    chi_st = chi0
    hist = []
    last_ok = None
    while steps > 0:
        chi = chi_profile(chi_st, z_st)
        Tn, ok = newton_flamelet(chi, T, y_fu)
        tmax = max(Tn)
        if not ok or tmax < 1000.0:
            break
        T = Tn; last_ok = (chi_st, tmax, list(Tn))
        hist.append((chi_st, tmax))
        chi_st *= growth; steps -= 1
    lo, hi = last_ok[0], chi_st
    base = last_ok[2]
    for _ in range(40):                      # squeeze the last burning solution with a geometric bisection
        mid = math.sqrt(lo * hi)
        Tn, ok = newton_flamelet(chi_profile(mid, z_st), base, y_fu)
        if ok and max(Tn) > 1000.0: lo, base = mid, Tn
        else: hi = mid
    return z_st, hist, lo, base

if __name__ == "__main__":
    z_st, hist, chi_q, Tq = extinction_scan(1.0)
    print("Z_st = %.4f   T_ad(Burke-Schumann) = %.1f K" % (z_st, max(burke_schumann(1.0)[1])))
    print("  chi_st [1/s]    T_max [K]   drop [K]")
    tad = hist[0][1]
    for c, t in hist[::3]:
        print("    %9.4f    %8.1f    %7.1f" % (c, t, tad - t))
    print("    %9.4f    %8.1f    %7.1f   <- quench" % (chi_q, max(Tq), tad - max(Tq)))
    print("chi range %.4f -> %.4f = x%.1f,  T_max drop = %.1f %%"
          % (hist[0][0], chi_q, chi_q / hist[0][0], 100.0 * (tad - max(Tq)) / tad))
    print()
    print("  Y_F1     Z_st    T_ad [K]   chi_q [1/s]")
    for yf in (1.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.125):
        zs, h, cq, _ = extinction_scan(yf)
        print("   %5.3f   %6.4f    %7.1f    %9.3f" % (yf, zs, max(burke_schumann(yf)[1]), cq))
```

Drive the dot right along the orange branch, cross the red line, and it drops to the floor. Pull $\chi_{st}$ back to the left and it does not come back up. The frozen solution is a steady solution too — hysteresis is the essence of this curve.

Look at how flat the first six rows are. Over a 90-fold rise in $\chi_{st}$ the temperature gave up 94.8 K. The last single step takes the remaining 203 K at once. That is the knee of the S-curve.

Put $s=4$ and $Y_{O,0}=0.232$ into $Z_{st}=1/(1+s Y_{F,1}/Y_{O,0})$. With $Y_{F,1}=1$ the denominator is $1+4/0.232$, and its reciprocal is the printed $Z_{st}=0.0548$. With $Y_{F,1}=0.125$ the same formula returns 0.3169 from the second table. Burke–Schumann at 2273.2 K and the $\chi_{st}=0.05$ solution at 2258.8 K are already 14.4 K apart. That gap is the infinite-rate limit versus the first burning solution.

From $\chi_{st}=0.0500$ to 4.5079 the temperature only falls from 2258.8 K to 2164.0 K, the 94.8 K already noted. The single step to 10.1232 takes the rest, down to 1960.9 K, and the table's drop of 297.9 K is just those two pieces. Along 0.1230, 0.3027, 0.7447, and 1.8322 the drops are 7.7 K, 13.4 K, 25.8 K, and 47.9 K. Nearly a straight line until the knee. Multiplying by 1.35 each time walks across that last bin coarsely. Forty geometric bisections are what pin $\chi_q=10.123$.

## Why does diluting the fuel break the limit so much harder

The second table in the output shows it. Diluting the fuel mass fraction from 1.0 to 0.125, a factor of 8, pulled the adiabatic flame temperature from 2273.2 K to 1719.8 K, down 24%. The quench limit $\chi_q$ went from 10.123 to 0.561, down by a factor of 18.

A 24% drop in temperature bought an 1800% drop in the limit. The Arrhenius term explains it. The reaction rate hangs on $\exp(-T_a/T)$ with $T_a = 24000$ K here. Moving $T$ from 2273 to 1720 moves the exponent from $-10.6$ to $-14.0$. A gap of 3.4 in the exponent is a factor of 30 in the product.

The shift of $Z_{st}$ from 0.055 to 0.317 pushes the same way. Slide `Z_st` to the right in the first simulation and you can see it. The peak of $\chi(Z)$ sits near $Z=0.5$, so a larger $Z_{st}$ puts the reaction zone closer to that peak. The same strain rate now hits it with more dissipation.

Exhaust gas recirculation is where this combination shows up in practice. Dilute the fuel or the oxidizer with an inert and $T_{ad}$ eases down while the quench limit collapses. Local extinction appears first, then flame lift-off, and blow-off last.

## Query the right side of the knee and the temperature falls to the inlet

Fill the $\chi_{st}$ grid out to $\chi_q=10.123$ and the table is dense, but the step from 4.5079 to 10.1232 drops the temperature from 2164.0 K to 1960.9 K, 203 K in one bin. How many points get spent on that knee is the actual trade. A cell with $\chi_{st} > \chi_q$ is not a lookup. The model chooses a frozen state, an extinction probability, or an unsteady flamelet.

$\chi_{st}$ itself comes out of the turbulence model. An index off by a factor of two flips which side of the knee is being read. A run that lands at inlet temperature should be checked at the $\chi_{st}$ input before the mechanism. One tridiagonal sweep behind that table costs the same kind of work as [Thomas elimination for implicit diffusion](/en/posts/2026-05-04-implicit-diffusion-thomas-algorithm/).
