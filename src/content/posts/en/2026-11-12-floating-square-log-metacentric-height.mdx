## Toss a square timber into water. Which face ends up on top?

Take a long timber with a square cross-section. Its density is half that of water. How does it float? Most people picture one face pointing up, flat and level. In reality it floats as a diamond, one corner pointing straight down. This post explains why with a single length: the metacentric height (a measure of a floating body's stability). By the end you will have derived the GM formula and computed the center of buoyancy at every heel angle, pinning down the density range where a square log floats flat.

My old notes from a gas dynamics course, section 3-6 "Buoyancy & stability," hold a few sketches and two terse lines. "Clockwise moment: stable." "Heavier on top: counterclockwise moment, unstable." Those two lines are today's starting point. A high center of gravity being unstable matches intuition. But a homogeneous log has its center of gravity dead center. So what picks the orientation?

## The center of buoyancy does not move with the body

Two forces act on the body. Weight pulls down through the center of gravity G. Buoyancy pushes up through B, the centroid of the submerged volume (the center of buoyancy). At equilibrium they are equal and lie on one vertical line.

Tilt the body a little. G is fixed to the body and rotates with it. B is not. One side sinks deeper, the other rises, and the submerged shape changes. B slides toward the deeper side. If it slides far enough, buoyancy pushes up outboard of G and rights the body. If not, weight and buoyancy form a couple (two equal, opposite forces) that increases the tilt.

The horizontal distance between the two lines of action is the righting arm GZ, positive when it restores. Try it in the simulation below.

## Metacentric height GM = BM − BG

At a small heel angle θ, the shifted line of buoyancy crosses the body's centerline at one point, the metacenter M. If M sits above G, the body is stable. The distance between them is GM, and for small angles the righting arm is

$$GZ \approx GM \sin\theta$$

GM follows from geometry alone. Let $I$ be the second moment of area of the waterplane (the section cut by the free surface) about its axis, and $V$ the submerged volume.

$$GM = BM - BG = \frac{I}{V} - (KG - KB)$$

$K$ is the keel, $KB$ the height of the center of buoyancy above it, and $KG$ the height of the center of gravity. $BM = I/V$ measures how far B slides as the body tilts. A wider waterplane makes it larger.

## For a square, GM depends on s alone

Take a rectangular section of width $b$ and height $h$, per unit length. For a homogeneous body with density ratio $s = \rho_{body}/\rho_{water}$, the draft (submerged depth) is $d = sh$.

$$I = \frac{b^3}{12},\quad V = b\,s h,\quad KB = \frac{sh}{2},\quad KG = \frac{h}{2}$$

Substituting gives

$$GM = \frac{b^2}{12\,s\,h} + \frac{s h}{2} - \frac{h}{2}$$

The first term is the restoring effect of the waterplane. The other two are the penalty for G sitting above B. For a square ($b = h = 1$) this collapses to

$$GM = \frac{6s^2 - 6s + 1}{12\,s}$$

The numerator vanishes at $s = (3 \pm \sqrt{3})/6$, that is 0.2113 and 0.7887. Between them GM is negative. Only very light or very heavy logs float flat. The sign is symmetric under $s \leftrightarrow 1-s$. Flip a body floating at density $s$ upside down and the part above water behaves like a body of density $1-s$ submerged.

This formula holds only for small θ. When GM is negative, it says nothing about how far the body rolls before stopping. That takes the full GZ(θ) curve at large angles.

## Code: bisecting the waterline at 901 heel angles

The procedure is simple. Rotate the section by θ. Find the water level $c$ that makes the submerged area equal $s\,bh$ by bisection (halving the bracket until the root is pinned). The centroid of the submerged polygon is B. With G at the origin, $GZ = -x_B$. Sweep θ from 0 to 90°; wherever GZ goes from negative to positive is a stable equilibrium.

```python
import numpy as np

def clip_below(poly, c):
    """part of the polygon with y < c (Sutherland–Hodgman, one line)"""
    out = []
    n = len(poly)
    for i in range(n):
        p, q = poly[i], poly[(i + 1) % n]
        pin, qin = p[1] < c, q[1] < c
        if pin:
            out.append(p)
        if pin != qin:
            t = (c - p[1]) / (q[1] - p[1])
            out.append((p[0] + t * (q[0] - p[0]), c))
    return out

def area_centroid(poly):
    A = cx = cy = 0.0
    n = len(poly)
    for i in range(n):
        x0, y0 = poly[i]; x1, y1 = poly[(i + 1) % n]
        cr = x0 * y1 - x1 * y0
        A += cr; cx += (x0 + x1) * cr; cy += (y0 + y1) * cr
    A *= 0.5
    return A, cx / (6 * A), cy / (6 * A)

def righting_arm(theta, s, b=1.0, h=1.0):
    """righting arm GZ at heel theta (G at origin, positive = restoring)"""
    co, si = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    box = [(-b/2, -h/2), (b/2, -h/2), (b/2, h/2), (-b/2, h/2)]
    poly = [(x*co - y*si, x*si + y*co) for x, y in box]
    target = s * b * h
    lo, hi = -0.8*(b+h), 0.8*(b+h)
    for _ in range(60):                       # bisect the waterline height c
        c = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        sub = clip_below(poly, c)
        A = area_centroid(sub)[0] if len(sub) >= 3 else 0.0
        lo, hi = (c, hi) if A < target else (lo, c)
    _, xB, _ = area_centroid(clip_below(poly, c))
    return -xB

def floating_angles(s, b=1.0, h=1.0, n=901):
    th = np.linspace(0, np.pi/2, n)
    gz = np.array([righting_arm(t, s, b, h) for t in th])
    gm = (righting_arm(1e-4, s, b, h)) / 1e-4
    stable = [0.0] if gm > 0 else []
    for i in range(1, n - 2):                 # GZ changes − → + : stable equilibrium
        if gz[i] < -1e-9 and gz[i+1] > -1e-9:
            stable.append(np.degrees(th[i] - gz[i]*(th[i+1]-th[i])/(gz[i+1]-gz[i])))
    if b != h and gz[-2] < 0:                 # 90° (standing pose) is also an equilibrium
        stable.append(90.0)
    return gm, stable

print("square section (b/h = 1), homogeneous body, s = rho_body / rho_water")
print(" s     GM_theory   GM_numeric   stable heel (deg)")
for s in [0.10, 0.20, 0.22, 0.24, 0.30, 0.50, 0.70, 0.76, 0.80, 0.90]:
    gm_th = 1/(12*s) + s/2 - 0.5
    gm, st = floating_angles(s)
    print(f"{s:.2f}  {gm_th:+.5f}    {gm:+.5f}     {', '.join(f'{a:.1f}' for a in st)}")
print()
print("s = 0.5, widening the section (h = 1)")
print(" b/h   GM_theory   stable heel (deg)")
for b in [1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.25, 1.3, 1.5]:
    s = 0.5
    gm_th = b*b/(12*s) + s/2 - 0.5
    gm, st = floating_angles(s, b=b)
    print(f"{b:.2f}  {gm_th:+.5f}    {', '.join(f'{a:.1f}' for a in st)}")
print(f"\ncritical s: (3-sqrt3)/6 = {(3-np.sqrt(3))/6:.4f}, (3+sqrt3)/6 = {(3+np.sqrt(3))/6:.4f}")
```

## Reading the table: 45°, 23.5°, and b/h = 1.225

First, numerical and theoretical GM agree to five digits. The small-angle slope $dGZ/d\theta$ is exactly $I/V - BG$. There is no approximation in the formula. There is only the small-angle condition.

Second, at s = 0.30, 0.50, and 0.70, 45° is the only stable point. By symmetry, GZ is always zero at 45° for a square. When GM is negative enough, a log pushed off 0° rolls to the next symmetric pose, the diamond.

Third, just inside the bounds, s = 0.22 and 0.24 stop at intermediate angles, 13.7° and 23.5°. The 76.3° and 66.5° entries are the same poses measured from the opposite face. When the flat position loses stability, the log does not jump to 45°. The tilt grows continuously from 0° and reaches 45° near s ≈ 0.28 (the same code gives 41.2° at s = 0.28 and 45.0° at 0.29). The sign of GM only tells you stable or unstable. The full GZ curve decides where the body stops.

Fourth, keep the density at 0.5 and widen the section. GM crosses zero at $b^2/6 = 1/4$, or $b/h = \sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.225$. That is the sign change between 1.20 and 1.25 in the table. A 22% wider section separates the diamond from the flat pose.

## Where this calculation shows up in ship design and CFD

Ship stability assessment is the code above applied to a real hull. Instead of a 2D section, a 3D hull is cut by the waterplane and a GZ curve is plotted against heel angle. Regulations demand a minimum GM and also a minimum area under the GZ curve (the righting energy). As today's table shows, small-angle GM alone cannot predict large-angle behavior.

In floating-body CFD that couples VOF (volume of fluid, which tracks the interface with a volume fraction) with six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body motion, the same calculation is the first check. Place the body on still water and see whether the simulation finds the right static pose. If not, the mass, inertia, or center-of-gravity input is usually wrong. Once pressure relaxes to hydrostatic $p = \rho g (c - y)$, the buoyancy and B must match today's polygon calculation. How the stress in a fluid at rest reduces to $-p\mathbf{I}$ is worked out from Newton's second law in [the post on the stress divergence ∇·τ](/en/posts/2026-11-03-stress-divergence-variable-viscosity-laplacian/).

Free-decay tests use the same GM. At small angles, roll motion is a pendulum, $I_{roll}\ddot\theta = -m g\,GM\,\theta$. The measured natural period backs out GM. A one-page hydrostatic calculation becomes the benchmark for a dynamic simulation. Unlike [the Borda–Carnot loss in a sudden expansion](/en/posts/2026-11-05-borda-carnot-sudden-expansion-lost-velocity/), where the answer requires a momentum balance, here integrating hydrostatic pressure closes the problem.

## One inequality to check before loading a barge

$$\frac{b^2}{12\,s\,h} > \frac{h}{2} - \frac{sh}{2} + \Delta_G$$

$\Delta_G$ is the rise in the center of gravity due to cargo. The left side grows with the square of the beam. The right side grows every time G goes up. Stacking cargo on deck raises $\Delta_G$. Light cargo lowers $s$, which raises the left side too. This single line tells you which one wins. In the first simulation, set b/h = 1.25 and raise G shift by just +0.1 to watch the section capsize.
