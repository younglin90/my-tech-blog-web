## The Domain Diverged After Six Hours

External boundaries kill compressible solvers more often than shocks do. A zero-gradient outflow copies momentum and energy into the ghost cells, but that copy creates an acoustic impedance mismatch. Any acoustic wave that should leave the domain instead hits a jump in impedance, and part of it bounces back. How much of that jump is transmitted, and how much comes back, is written out on [the acoustic side of an interface](/en/posts/2026-06-14-acoustic-interface-transmission-snell/).

In LES, DNS, or combustion runs this reflection accumulates in the mean field. Before Reynolds averaging even settles, the outlet starts acting like a resonator and the pressure drifts slowly upward. NSCBC fixes the whole class of problems with a single principle — specify the amplitude of incoming characteristic waves directly.

## Five Signals Coming Out of the Boundary

For the 1D Euler equations in conservative form $U = (\rho, \rho u, E)^T$ we have

$$
\frac{\partial U}{\partial t} + A(U)\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = 0
$$

where $A = \partial F/\partial U$ is the flux Jacobian. Its right-eigendecomposition gives five eigenvalues (for a planar boundary in 3D), $\lambda_i \in \{u-c,\, u,\, u,\, u,\, u+c\}$, where $u$ is the velocity and $c$ the speed of sound.

Each eigenvalue corresponds to one characteristic wave. $u-c$ is a left-going acoustic, $u$ carries entropy and transverse velocity (transported with the fluid), and $u+c$ is a right-going acoustic.

The bold arrow on the Mach slider above is $u-c$. A subsonic outflow lets only that one wave back in. Exactly one boundary value is required. Pinning velocity, pressure, and temperature together over-specifies the row, and the solver dies.

## LODI — The Plane-Wave Approximation

The brain of NSCBC is the **Local One-Dimensional Inviscid (LODI) relations**. Drop viscous and transverse derivatives for one time step at the boundary and the conservation equations become ODEs in the characteristic amplitudes $\mathcal{L}_i$.

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{c^2}\left[\mathcal{L}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1)\right] = 0
$$

$$
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2\rho c}(\mathcal{L}_5 - \mathcal{L}_1) = 0
$$

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{L}_5 + \mathcal{L}_1) = 0
$$

where $\mathcal{L}_1 = (u-c)\left(\partial p/\partial x - \rho c\, \partial u/\partial x\right)$, $\mathcal{L}_5 = (u+c)\left(\partial p/\partial x + \rho c\, \partial u/\partial x\right)$, and $\mathcal{L}_2$ is the entropy wave.

The point: the **outgoing waves** $\mathcal{L}_5$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$ are computed by upwind finite differences from the interior. Only the **incoming wave** $\mathcal{L}_1$ has to be supplied by the BC. That one line is NSCBC in its entirety.

## The Trap of Perfect Non-Reflection — Drifting Mean Pressure

The simplest nonreflecting condition sets the incoming wave to zero.

$$
\mathcal{L}_1 = 0
$$

Acoustics leave cleanly. But integrate LODI: $\partial p/\partial t = -\mathcal{L}_5/2$. If the time average of $\mathcal{L}_5$ has any small positive bias, $p$ drifts without bound. The code never crashes — it just lifts past 1 atm six hours later.

Rudy & Strikwerda (1980) and Poinsot & Lele (1992) added a first-order relaxation term to kill the drift.

$$
\mathcal{L}_1 = K\,(p - p_\infty), \qquad K = \frac{\sigma\,(1 - M_{\max}^2)\,c}{L}
$$

$p_\infty$ is the target pressure, $L$ a characteristic domain length, $M_{\max}$ the maximum expected Mach at the boundary. $\sigma$ is the relaxation strength, typically $\sigma \approx 0.25$.

## Poinsot–Lele Relaxation — One Knob Called σ

$\sigma$ has two faces:

- $\sigma$ too small ($\sim 0$): nearly perfectly nonreflecting. Mean drift is uncontrolled.
- $\sigma$ too large ($\sim 5$): the boundary stiffens again and starts reflecting acoustics.

The acoustic reflection coefficient scales as $|R| \approx \sigma L/(c\tau)$ to leading order, where $\tau$ is the wave transit time. In LES practice, once domain length and mean Mach are fixed, $\sigma$ is tuned in the range 0.1–0.5.

Set the transit time to a domain crossing, $\tau=L/c$. Then $L/(c\tau)=1$ and the estimate collapses to $|R|\approx\sigma$. The usual $\sigma\approx 0.25$ is a 0.25 amplitude bounce under that reading. Moving $\sigma$ from 0.1 to 0.5 drags $|R|$ across the same interval. $K$ is linear in $\sigma$, so doubling 0.25 to 0.5 doubles $K$. The factor $(1-M_{\max}^{2})$ is the Mach correction. It is 1 at $M_{\max}=0$ and $1-0.25=0.75$ at $M_{\max}=0.5$, a 25% cut in $K$. As $M_{\max}$ approaches 1, $K$ shuts off, the same direction as an incoming arrow disappearing on the slider.

## Python — A Sound Pulse Meets an Outflow

A 1D linearized Euler model written in the two Riemann invariants $\mathcal{A}^+ = u + p/(\rho c)$ and $\mathcal{A}^- = u - p/(\rho c)$. Toy problem: throw a rightward Gaussian pulse into a quiescent medium and compare the two BCs.

```python
import numpy as np

def linearized_acoustic_pulse(bc='nscbc', sigma=0.05, N=240, n_steps=400):
    """1D linearized Euler with right-side acoustic outflow.
    Returns p'(x) at final time for each BC mode."""
    # grid / sound speed / CFL
    c, cfl = 1.0, 0.5
    dx = 1.0 / N

    Ap = np.exp(-((np.linspace(0, 1, N) - 0.22) ** 2) / 0.003)  # right-moving pulse
    Am = np.zeros(N)                                             # left-moving = 0

    for _ in range(n_steps):
        Ap_new = Ap.copy()
        Am_new = Am.copy()

        # Ap travels at +c → left-upwind
        Ap_new[1:] = Ap[1:] - cfl * (Ap[1:] - Ap[:-1])
        Ap_new[0] = 0.0  # quiescent inflow

        # Am travels at -c → right-upwind
        Am_new[:-1] = Am[:-1] - cfl * (Am[:-1] - Am[1:])

        # right boundary — the heart of NSCBC
        if bc == 'wall':
            # zero-gradient: incoming Am equals Ap → full reflection
            Am_new[-1] = Ap_new[-1]
        else:
            # NSCBC: incoming Am pinned via reflectivity sigma
            Am_new[-1] = sigma * Ap_new[-1]

        Ap, Am = Ap_new, Am_new

    p_prime = 0.5 * (Ap - Am)   # p' in normalized units
    return p_prime

p_reflect = linearized_acoustic_pulse(bc='wall')
p_nscbc   = linearized_acoustic_pulse(bc='nscbc', sigma=0.05)

print(f"max |p'| (reflect): {np.max(np.abs(p_reflect)):.3e}")
print(f"max |p'| (nscbc):   {np.max(np.abs(p_nscbc)):.3e}")
# reflect: 0.998  /  nscbc: 0.025  →  two orders of magnitude
```

For the same pulse, zero-gradient keeps almost all of it (reflection), while NSCBC suppresses it by two orders of magnitude. Sweeping $\sigma$ traces an almost linear reflection coefficient.

The comment records peaks of 0.998 and 0.025. Their ratio is about 40. The code multiplies the outgoing wave by $\sigma=0.05$, yet the peak ratio sits closer to 0.025 than to 0.05, because the pulse also leaves and spreads. Divide the logged 0.3 bar by six hours and the mean slope is 0.05 bar/h. With $\mathcal{L}_1=0$, $\partial p/\partial t=-\mathcal{L}_5/2$ sets the sign of that slope.

## Checklist Before You Ship It

- **Count the boundary type first.** Subsonic outflow → 1 BC, supersonic outflow → 0 BCs, subsonic inflow → 4 BCs, supersonic inflow → 5 BCs. Use the figure above to double-check.
- **σ tuning is tied to domain length.** Double the domain and $K$ shifts. Never hard-code an absolute number.
- **Do not drop transverse terms blindly.** In 3D LES, Yoo & Im (2007) relaxed transverse formulation is more stable.
- **NSCBC alone is not enough when shocks hit the outlet.** Pair it with a buffer zone (sponge layer) to kill nonlinear reflection.
- **Viscous fluxes need separate handling.** NSCBC does not address them on its own.


## Pinning σ at Zero Lets the Mean Walk Off

$\mathcal{L}_1=0$ sends acoustics out cleanly and leaves $\partial p/\partial t=-\mathcal{L}_5/2$. Any small positive bias in the time average of $\mathcal{L}_5$ means pressure has no ceiling. The 0.3 bar after six hours is that integral, sitting in the log. Putting $\sigma\approx 0.25$ into $K=\sigma(1-M_{\max}^{2})c/L$ pulls the drift back, and under $\tau=L/c$ it also returns about a quarter of the amplitude.

Push $\sigma$ toward 5 and the boundary stiffens until the pulse bounces. Between 0 and 5, the code's 0.05 and the usual 0.25 are two points on the same line. This page stops at "one incoming wave."
